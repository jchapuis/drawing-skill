# The quadruped as a solid

The usual failure with an animal is a portrait with scenery attached. The head
gets built because it is the only part you have a construction for, and
everything from the neck back gets outlined. Outlining is the cardinal error. The
`--masses` gate describes the result in a few words: the subject reads as *one
connected animal, poll to hoof*, and the drawing as *a head, a detached slab, and
floating legs*. Even after the heads are rebuilt, the worst box in the inventory
(measured by structure rather than by ink) was still a cow's chest in the one case
measured, and so was the third worst.

This applies to any four-legged animal, not only cattle. Cattle are the worked
example below; dog and cat heads have a section of their own.

## The schema is a diagnostic, never a scaffold

Everything below describes the **average** animal, and the average animal is not
the one in front of you. **Do not build on it.** Build on landmarks measured off
the subject. Use what follows afterwards, to ask why your measured animal differs
from the average and whether the difference is the breed, the pose, the
individual or a mistake.

The risk is real. A head built on a computed ball, with every feature then
measured honestly and hung on it, comes out with every box correct and is not a
face. A barrel, a shoulder wedge and four zigzag legs are the same trap in a hide.
If a passage below could be followed with the subject covered up, you are using
it wrongly. `[read: source]` marks a claim taken from the named book or
literature: it has not been tested here. Rules without the tag come from
measured failures.

## The eight ways a drawn quadruped fails

Each of these can pass `--parts` and the silhouette check, so each is given with
the measurement that does catch it.

| The fault | The measurement that finds it |
|---|---|
| **The animals have no bodies**: solidly built heads sitting over pale open ground | `--masses`: the subject reads as *one connected animal, poll to hoof*; the drawing as *a head, a detached slab, and floating legs* |
| **The chest is where it breaks**: not the legs, not the barrel, but the passage from neck to foreleg | Structure share in the chest box drops to around **0.44-0.57 of the subject's**, and those come out the worst boxes in the whole inventory |
| **Legs that never close into legs**: contours below the brisket crossing each other, so the pale runs on down to the ground undivided | The subject shows a leg, a gap, and a leg. Measure the gap |
| **The heads are eggs**: domed across the poll, bulging widest in the middle | The subject's is a **shield**: broad and nearly flat across the poll, then two long near-straight runs falling inward to a narrow jaw. Its widest point is at the very top |
| **Horns as wire antennae**, often twice the subject's length, with no body | They are cones. Measure the width at the poll against the height they rise clear of it; the ratio is closer to 1:1.3 than to a sliver |
| **Ears as lobes** | They are **leaves that come to a point** at the outer end |
| **A blaze painted as the whole face**, the dark side panels reaching only a third of the way in | A blaze is a strip down the middle; the dark comes **most** of the way in, or each face reads as a white head with two smudges |
| **The furthest animal carries the densest line** | Sight the ink share of a far animal against a near one. It should be lighter, and the drawing routinely has it heavier |

An outline can be right while the order of widths inside it is wrong, and no
mechanical check sees that. A blind describer asked the three questions **of the
head crop alone** does.

## The animal is one mass before it is any parts

Head, neck, chest, barrel and legs are **one continuous solid** that the light
crosses and the contour runs around. The place this is usually lost is the
**chest**, where the neck's cylinder turns into the ribcage and the forelegs hang
off it.

That junction is hard because nothing in it is a shape you can name. The head is
a form and the leg is a column, and the passage between them only reads if you
have decided how the neck *enters* the chest and where the point of shoulder
juts out of it. Skip the decision and you draw the head, draw the legs and leave
the gap open, which is what produces the low chest score in the table above.

So **block the barrel and the chest at the same stage as the head, and check
them together.** Do not draw the head first. A head at contour over a chest at
block-in fails the gate, because no part may be more than one stage ahead of any
other.

## The skeleton under the hide [read: Ellenberger & Baum; Bammes]

The surface contour of an animal is bone showing through: the withers ridge,
the hip's sharp angle, the shoulder blade's edge. Ellenberger and Baum's *Atlas
of Animal Anatomy for Artists* maps this, and Bammes teaches the same animals as
**basic forms** first. Neither has been tested here; both say the following.

- **The ribcage is a barrel**, the same egg you draw for a human thorax, rotated
  to lie horizontal. It is the animal's largest single form and it is deep
  rather than round: a cow's is deep and slab-sided, a sighthound's is deep and
  narrow. **Measure its depth against its length** before you draw
  a line of it.
- **There is no clavicle.** The shoulder blade is not attached to the skeleton by
  a joint: it **floats in a muscular sling against the outside of the ribcage** and slides
  along it as the animal moves. The whole front end hangs between the two blades
  the way a bucket hangs in a rope. Two consequences for drawing: the withers
  ridge is the blades' upper edges standing proud of the spine, and the front leg
  has no fixed pivot: its top slides, so a foreleg's angle at the shoulder
  cannot be worked out from theory, only observed.
- **The pelvis is structurally the same as yours**, tilted to lie along a
  horizontal spine. On cattle its two landmarks are visible through the hide and
  are the fixed points you hang the hindquarters on: the **hooks** (the outer hip
  bones, standing out at the top of the flank) and the **pins** (the rump's rear
  corners, slightly lower). Livestock judging reads a cow off exactly these.
- **The head extends forward from the neck rather than balancing on top of it.**
  The neck is a cylinder leaving the chest at an angle, not a stalk rising from
  shoulders.

## The leg, where drawings go backwards [read: Laws; vet anatomy]

**The joint halfway down the hind leg that looks like a knee bending the wrong
way is the hock, and the hock is an ankle.** It is the equivalent of your heel,
held permanently off the ground. Nothing on the animal bends backwards. The
veterinary and naturalist literature states this repeatedly, and it is the most
common error artists make with quadrupeds.

The true knee, the **stifle**, is up inside the flank, level with the belly
line, usually inside the body outline entirely. The same is true in front: the
**elbow** sits down against the bottom of the ribcage, and the joint you can see
halfway down the front leg is the **carpus**, the wrist.

| What you see | What it actually is | Where the real joint is |
|---|---|---|
| "knee", front leg, mid-height | **carpus**, the wrist | The **elbow** is against the lower ribcage, often inside the silhouette |
| "backwards knee", hind leg | **hock**, the ankle or heel | The **stifle** (the knee) is up in the flank at belly level |
| The whole lower leg | **hand and foot bones**, hugely elongated: the cannon is one fused metacarpal/metatarsal | — |
| The "ankle" just above the hoof | the **fetlock**, a knuckle | — |
| The hoof | a **toenail**, and the animal stands on its tip | — |

John Muir Laws' comparative-leg sheet gives a practical test that is a
measurement, not a guess: **on an unguligrade animal the wrist sits
halfway between the elbow and the hoof**, and the heel sits midway along the
visible hind leg. Plantigrade animals (bears, humans) put the whole sole down and
the wrist reaches the ground; digitigrade ones (dogs, cats) carry the wrist just
above it. So measure the height of the mid-leg joint against the leg's total
length and the posture follows. Do not infer the posture from the species and
then place the joint to match.

In practice the hind leg is a **zigzag of four
runs**: a short hidden femur going down and forward, a long tibia going
down and back to the hock, the cannon dropping vertically, the pastern angling
forward to the hoof. The drive comes from the top of the zigzag, inside the body,
so a hind leg reads as powerful only when the flank mass above it is drawn as
part of the leg.

## The head (cattle)

A bovine head is a **muzzle box** hung on a **cranial mass**, and both are
squarer than you will draw them. The error is predictable: you will draw an
**egg**, domed across the top and widest in the middle, where the subject has a
**shield**: broad and near-flat across the poll, then two long **near-straight
runs** falling inward to a narrow jaw. That is a wedge with a flat top, and its
widest point is at the very top, not in the middle.

The bony reason, from the veterinary literature [read: vet anatomy] and untested
here: cattle have
**wide, robust frontal bones** that form the flat plate of the poll, with the
orbits set out at the sides of it rather than facing forward, and the **cornual
processes projecting from the frontal bone near its rear edge**. So the horns
grow out of the corners of a flat plate, which fixes their spacing and
why they cannot be moved inward to make a nicer silhouette.

Measure each of these off the subject; an unmeasured guess is usually wrong:

- **Horns are cones.** Measure the width where they leave the poll against the
  height they rise clear of it; the ratio is closer to 1:1.3 than to a sliver (in one case about 14px across at
  the poll, rising about 18 clear). Drawn as slivers they come out as wire antennae, often twice the subject's
  length. A horn has a base.
- **Ears are leaves that come to a point** at the outer end, set below and
  outside the horns. Drawn as lobes they read as loops.
- **The eye is a white slot**, much wider than deep (in one case about 16
  across by 6 deep, the same on all six animals), cut into the dark of the face,
  with a pupil about 6 wide centred in it and touching the upper lid. It reads as a heavy-lidded
  letterbox, not an almond.
- **The cheek around the muzzle matters more than the muzzle pad's width.** In one
  case the pads measured 55, 46, 46 and 48 wide across four animals, and what was
  still wrong after the rebuild was the white cheek, not their size. Measure how
  much white cheek the subject leaves either side of the muzzle. If the drawing
  leaves less, the muzzle ties with the blaze for attention instead of the
  blaze winning the head.
- **The blaze is a strip down the middle.** The dark side panels must come
  **most** of the way in; at a third of the way in each face reads as a white
  head with two smudges.

## The head (dogs and cats)

A dog's or a cat's head is a **rounded skull** with a **muzzle** set on its
front, and the two meet at the **stop**: the step down from the forehead to the
bridge of the nose, at or just below the eyes. The likeness of one animal
against another of the same kind lives almost entirely in how those three
relate, so this is where a drawing goes generic first. Ellenberger, Baum and
Dittrich's *An Atlas of Animal Anatomy for Artists* shows the dog and cat skulls
under the coat, and Ken Hultgren's *The Art of Animal Drawing* builds both heads
from a ball for the cranium with a smaller block or wedge for the muzzle
[read: Ellenberger et al.; Hultgren]. In paraphrase, both say the following.

- **The skull.** The cranium is a ball, widest across the cheekbones (the
  zygomatic arches) rather than at the crown. Under a heavy coat its outline is
  fur, so place it from the eyes and the ear bases, not from the silhouette.
  A cat's cranium is large against its face; a dog's ranges from round to long
  and narrow with the breed.
- **The stop.** Its depth and height decide the profile: a deep stop gives a
  domed forehead over a separate muzzle, a shallow one a nearly straight line
  from crown to nose. In a three-quarter view it shows as the place where the
  bridge of the nose leaves the forehead between the eyes.
- **The muzzle.** On a dog, a block or wedge running forward from the stop to
  the nose, with the upper lip (the flews) hanging at its sides and the lower
  jaw tucked under it. It is narrower than the skull and usually tapers. On a
  cat it is short: two rounded whisker pads under a small nose, with a small
  chin below, barely projecting from the face.
- **How the ears join.** An ear grows from the top or the side of the cranium,
  never from the cheek, and its base is wider than it looks. A dropped ear
  (most retrievers, spaniels, hounds) folds at its base and hangs from it, so
  the fold is a hard edge high on the skull and the flap falls over the side of
  the head. A pricked ear (cats, many dogs) stands on the top corner of the
  skull with its opening turned forward or out.
- **The eyes.** They sit in sockets under the brow, on the front of the head
  and angled outward a little on a dog, more forward on a cat. The lids follow
  the eyeball; a ring of dark lid or socket shadow is usually what reads, more
  than a drawn outline. Brows, lashes and a white round the iris are rarely
  visible and turn an animal's face human when drawn.

### Landmark ratios: measure them on the subject before drawing

Write these in `reading.md` with the pixel coordinates you read them from, in
the subject's view (three-quarter, profile or front). They are **measured on
the subject, never taken from a typical breed**: the point is how this animal
differs from the average one, and a ratio from a breed standard draws the
standard.

| ratio | measured as |
|---|---|
| eye spacing / head width | distance between the eye centres, over the skull's width at the eye line (cheek edge to cheek edge, or to where the far ear joins it; ear flaps and loose fur left out) |
| eye line to nose tip / head height | vertical distance from the eye line to the nose, over crown to chin |
| muzzle length / skull length | stop to nose tip, over stop to the back of the skull (in profile); in a front or three-quarter view, eye line to nose over crown to eye line |
| ear base position | where the ear's front edge leaves the skull, as a fraction of head height down from the crown and of head width from the near side |
| head width / chest width | skull width at the eye line, over the chest's widest point (in a lying or sitting pose, its width just below the jaw) |

A jaw that is too deep or too square is common in a drawn dog; when the head
looks boxy, measure the depth from the bottom of the nose to the chin over head
height as well.

To read them on the drawing, use the same landmarks:

- `--scan` on the head's box, from the side the face turns toward, prints the
  silhouette edge row by row for subject and drawing, which gives the skull's
  width at the eye line and the jaw's taper;
- `--overlay --box` on the head shows the two inks over each other: eye
  centres, the nose, the stop and the mouth line. Say for each subject line
  which drawn line is meant to be it and how far it runs off;
- the eye and nose centres are the dark blobs in each box; their positions can
  be right while the skull around them is too wide, which changes every ratio
  that divides by head width. That is the usual cause of a head that looks
  boxy with every feature in its place.

## A herd is individuals, and the check is arithmetic

Several animals drawn the same way are one animal stamped repeatedly, which the
rule against twinning forbids. **Write down each animal's turn before hanging
anything on it, and read the turn off the subject by where the muzzle sits inside
its own head.**

Record it as an offset in pixels, per animal (for example *head centre 691,
muzzle centre 673, eighteen left*), so the turn is data and not an impression.

Twinning is not two neighbours facing the same way; a real herd will often give
you two in a row turned the same direction. What matters is that **no two
neighbours have the same *amount* of turn**, and that tips are mixed in with the
yaws (one animal tipped back and away, one tipped down), so that each muzzle
tapers differently. Let about one animal in six look straight out.

Depth is the other axis, and it is the one most often drawn backwards: the
smallest, furthest animal ends up carrying the heaviest line in the picture.
**An animal one plane back takes a lighter weight and a duller value than one in
front. Settle depth before light when choosing weights.**

## The sub-forms an inventory descends to

An entry that stops at "the cow" or "the dog" gives the later checks one
silhouette to judge. Write an entry for each of these the subject shows, and
name the hidden ones in `_absent`:

```checklist
object: quadruped|animal|dog|puppy|cat|kitten|horse|pony|donkey|cow|bull|calf|ox|sheep|goat|pig|deer|fox|wolf|lion|tiger|bear
sub-forms: head ear eye muzzle|snout|nose neck body|barrel|torso leg foot|feet|hoof|hooves|paw tail
```

`check.py --checklist parts.json` reads this block and fails on any sub-form
the inventory neither names nor excuses in `_absent`.

## Build order

1. **Say the posture in writing** (plantigrade, digitigrade or unguligrade) and
   check it by measuring the mid-leg joint's height against the leg's length.
   A wrong posture puts every joint on the animal in the wrong place.
2. **The barrel**, as a measured depth against a measured length. It is the
   largest form and everything else is placed against it.
3. **The pelvis and the shoulder mass**, hung on the barrel: hooks and pins
   behind, the blade's ridge in front. Neither can be worked out from theory;
   both are landmarks you measure.
4. **The head's box and the neck's cylinder**, and the **chest** where the two
   meet, at the same stage, never after. This is the junction most often lost.
5. **The legs as zigzags between measured joints**, elbow and stifle placed
   *inside* the body outline where the subject puts them, hock and carpus at their
   measured mid-leg heights.
6. **The turn of each head, written down**, and the centre line placed where the
   turn puts it rather than down the middle of the box.
7. Horns, ears, eye slot and muzzle box last, each with a measured base and none
   of them a sliver.

Draw the animal **whole and at one stage throughout**. A finished head over a
blocked-in chest is not an animal in progress. The gate that catches it is
walking the inventory and refusing to pass when one part is a stage ahead of
another.
