# The quadruped as a solid

The usual failure with an animal is a head portrait with scenery attached. The
head gets built because it is the only part you have a construction for, and
everything from the neck back gets outlined. Outlining is the worst mistake you
can make here. The `--masses` gate describes the result in a few words: the
subject is *one connected animal, poll to hoof*, and the drawing is *a head, a
detached slab, and floating legs*. In the one case measured, even after the
heads were rebuilt, the worst box in the inventory (measured by structure, not
by ink) was still a cow's chest, and so was the third worst.

This applies to any four-legged animal. Cattle are the worked example below;
dog and cat heads have a section of their own.

## The schema is a diagnostic, never a scaffold

Everything below describes the **average** animal, which is not the one in
front of you. **Do not build on it.** Build on landmarks measured from the
subject, and use what follows only afterwards, to ask whether a difference from
the average comes from the breed, the pose, the individual or a mistake.

For example, a head built on a computed ball, with every feature then measured
honestly and hung on it, comes out with every box correct and is still not a
face. A barrel, a shoulder wedge and four zigzag legs are the same trap applied
to the body. If you could follow a passage below with the subject covered up,
you are using it wrongly. `[read: source]` marks a claim from the named book or
literature, not yet tested here; untagged rules come from measured failures.

## The eight ways a drawn quadruped fails

Each of these can pass `--parts` and the silhouette check, so each is listed
with the measurement that does catch it.

| The fault | The measurement that finds it |
|---|---|
| **The animals have no bodies**: solidly built heads over pale open ground | `--masses`: the subject is *one connected animal, poll to hoof*; the drawing is *a head, a detached slab, and floating legs* |
| **The chest is where it breaks**: not the legs or the barrel, but the passage from neck to foreleg | The structure share in the chest box drops to around **0.44-0.57 of the subject's**, and these are the worst boxes in the whole inventory |
| **Legs that never become legs**: contours below the brisket cross each other, so the pale runs on down to the ground undivided | The subject shows a leg, a gap, and a leg. Measure the gap |
| **The heads are eggs**: domed across the poll, widest in the middle | The subject's is a **shield**, widest at the very top (§ The head (cattle)) |
| **Horns as wire antennae**, often twice the subject's length, with no body | They are cones; measure base width against height (§ The head (cattle)) |
| **Ears as lobes** | They are **leaves that come to a point** at the outer end |
| **A blaze painted as the whole face**, with the dark side panels reaching only a third of the way in | A blaze is a strip down the middle; the dark comes **most** of the way in |
| **The furthest animal carries the densest line** | Compare the ink share of a far animal with a near one. It should be lighter, and drawings often have it heavier |

An outline can be right while the order of widths inside it is wrong, and no
mechanical check sees that. A blind describer asked the three questions **about
the head crop alone** does.

## The animal is one mass before it is any parts

Head, neck, chest, barrel and legs are **one continuous solid** that the light
crosses and the contour runs around. This is usually lost at the **chest**,
where the cylinder of the neck turns into the ribcage and the forelegs hang
from it.

That junction is hard because nothing in it has a shape you can name. The head
is a form and the leg is a column, but the passage between them only works if
you have decided how the neck *enters* the chest and where the point of the
shoulder juts out of it. If you skip that decision, you draw the head, draw the
legs, and leave the gap open, which produces the low chest score in the table
above.

So **block in the barrel and the chest at the same stage as the head, and check
them together.** Do not draw the head first. A head at the contour stage over a
chest at block-in fails the gate, because no part may be even one stage ahead
of any other.

## The skeleton under the hide [read: Ellenberger & Baum; Bammes]

An animal's surface contour is bone showing through: the ridge of the withers,
the sharp angle of the hip, the edge of the shoulder blade. Ellenberger and
Baum's *Atlas of Animal Anatomy for Artists* maps this, and Bammes teaches the
same animals as **basic forms** first. Both say the following.

- **The ribcage is a barrel**, the same egg you draw for a human thorax, turned
  to lie horizontal. It is the animal's largest single form, and it is deep
  more than round: a cow's is deep and slab-sided, a sighthound's deep and
  narrow. **Measure its depth against its length** before you draw any of it.
- **There is no clavicle.** No joint attaches the shoulder blade to the
  skeleton: it **hangs in a sling of muscle against the outside of the
  ribcage** and slides along it as the animal moves, so the whole front end
  hangs between the two blades like a bucket on a rope. This has two
  consequences for drawing. The withers ridge is the upper edges of the blades
  standing above the spine. And the front leg has no fixed pivot, because its
  top slides, so you must observe the foreleg's angle at the shoulder; you
  cannot work it out.
- **The pelvis has the same structure as yours**, tilted to lie along a
  horizontal spine. On cattle its two landmarks show through the hide and are
  the fixed points you hang the hindquarters on: the **hooks** (the outer hip
  bones, standing out at the top of the flank) and the **pins** (the rear
  corners of the rump, slightly lower). Livestock judges assess a cow by exactly
  these.
- **The head extends forward from the neck instead of balancing on top of it.**
  The neck is a cylinder leaving the chest at an angle, not a stalk rising from
  the shoulders.

## The leg, where drawings go backwards [read: Laws; vet anatomy]

**The joint halfway down the hind leg that looks like a knee bending the wrong
way is the hock, and the hock is an ankle.** It is the equivalent of your heel,
held permanently off the ground. Nothing on the animal bends backwards. The
veterinary and naturalist literature says this repeatedly, and it is the
commonest mistake artists make with quadrupeds.

The true knee, the **stifle**, is up in the flank at the level of the belly
line, usually entirely inside the body outline. The same holds in front: the
**elbow** sits against the bottom of the ribcage, and the joint you see halfway
down the front leg is the **carpus**, the wrist.

| What you see | What it actually is | Where the real joint is |
|---|---|---|
| "knee", front leg, mid-height | **carpus**, the wrist | The **elbow** is against the lower ribcage, often inside the silhouette |
| "backwards knee", hind leg | **hock**, the ankle or heel | The **stifle** (the knee) is up in the flank at belly level |
| The whole lower leg | **hand and foot bones**, hugely elongated: the cannon is one fused metacarpal/metatarsal | — |
| The "ankle" just above the hoof | the **fetlock**, a knuckle | — |
| The hoof | a **toenail**, and the animal stands on its tip | — |

John Muir Laws' comparative-leg sheet gives a practical test by measurement:
**on an unguligrade animal (one that stands on hooves) the wrist sits halfway
between the elbow and the hoof**, and the heel sits midway along the visible
hind leg. Plantigrade animals (bears, humans) put the whole sole down, so the
wrist reaches the ground; digitigrade ones (dogs, cats) walk on their toes and
carry the wrist just above it. So measure the height of the mid-leg joint
against the leg's total length, and the posture follows. Do not infer the
posture from the species and then place the joint to match.

In practice the hind leg is a **zigzag of four runs**: a short hidden femur going
down and forward, a long tibia going down and back to the hock, the cannon
dropping vertically, and the pastern angling forward to the hoof. The drive
comes from the top of the zigzag, inside the body, so a hind leg looks powerful
only when the flank mass above it is drawn as part of the leg.

## The head (cattle)

A bovine head is a **muzzle box** hung on a **cranial mass**, and both are
squarer than you will draw them. You will tend to draw an **egg**, domed across
the top and widest in the middle, where the subject has a **shield**: broad and
nearly flat across the poll, then two long, **nearly straight runs** falling
inward to a narrow jaw. That is a wedge with a flat top, widest at the very top.

The bony reason [read: vet anatomy]: cattle have **wide, robust frontal bones**
that form the flat plate of the poll, with the eye sockets set out at its sides
instead of facing forward, and the **horn cores projecting from the frontal
bone near its rear edge**. The horns grow from the corners of a flat plate,
which fixes their spacing; do not move them inward for a nicer silhouette.

Measure each of these on the subject, because a guess is usually wrong:

- **Horns are cones, and a horn has a base.** Measure the width where they
  leave the poll against the height they rise clear of it. The ratio is closer
  to 1:1.3 than to a sliver (in one case about 14px across at the poll, rising
  about 18 clear). Drawn as slivers, they come out as wire antennae, often twice
  the subject's length.
- **Ears are leaves that come to a point** at the outer end, set below and
  outside the horns. Drawn as lobes, they look like loops.
- **The eye is a white slot**, much wider than deep (in one case about 16
  across by 6 deep, the same on all six animals), cut into the dark of the face,
  with a pupil about 6 wide centred in it and touching the upper lid. It looks
  like a heavy-lidded letterbox, not an almond.
- **The cheek around the muzzle matters more than the width of the muzzle
  pad.** In one case the pads measured 55, 46, 46 and 48 wide across four
  animals, and what was still wrong after the rebuild was the white cheek, not
  the pads' size. Measure how much white cheek the subject leaves on either side
  of the muzzle. If the drawing leaves less, the muzzle competes with the blaze
  for attention, where the blaze should dominate the head.
- **The blaze is a strip down the middle.** The dark side panels must come
  **most** of the way in; at a third of the way in, each face looks like a white
  head with two smudges.

## The head (dogs and cats)

A dog's or cat's head is a **rounded skull** with a **muzzle** set on its front,
and the two meet at the **stop**: the step down from the forehead to the bridge
of the nose, at or just below the eyes. What makes one animal look different
from another of its kind lies almost entirely in how these three relate, so this
is where a drawing goes generic first. Ellenberger, Baum and Dittrich's *An
Atlas of Animal Anatomy for Artists* shows the dog and cat skulls under the
coat, and Ken Hultgren's *The Art of Animal Drawing* builds both heads from a
ball for the cranium and a smaller block or wedge for the muzzle
[read: Ellenberger et al.; Hultgren]. In paraphrase, both say the following.

- **The skull.** The cranium is a ball, widest across the cheekbones (the
  zygomatic arches), not at the crown. Under a heavy coat its outline is fur, so
  place it from the eyes and the ear bases, not from the silhouette. A cat's
  cranium is large against its face; a dog's ranges from round to long and
  narrow depending on the breed.
- **The stop.** Its depth and height decide the profile: a deep stop gives a
  domed forehead over a separate muzzle, a shallow one a nearly straight line
  from crown to nose. In a three-quarter view it shows where the bridge of the
  nose leaves the forehead between the eyes.
- **The muzzle.** On a dog it is a block or wedge running forward from the stop
  to the nose, with the upper lip (the flews) hanging at its sides and the lower
  jaw tucked under it. It is narrower than the skull and usually tapers. On a
  cat it is short: two rounded whisker pads under a small nose, with a small
  chin below, barely projecting from the face.
- **How the ears join.** An ear grows from the top or side of the cranium, never
  from the cheek, and its base is wider than it looks. A dropped ear (most
  retrievers, spaniels, hounds) folds at its base and hangs from it, so the fold
  is a hard edge high on the skull and the flap falls over the side of the head.
  A pricked ear (cats, many dogs) stands on the top corner of the skull with its
  opening turned forward or out.
- **The eyes.** They sit in sockets under the brow, on the front of the head,
  angled slightly outward on a dog and more forward on a cat. The lids follow
  the eyeball. What shows is usually a ring of dark lid or socket shadow more
  than a drawn outline. Brows, lashes and a white around the iris are rarely
  visible, and drawing them makes an animal's face look human.

### Landmark ratios: measure them on the subject before drawing

Write these in `reading.md` with the pixel coordinates you read them from, in
the subject's view (three-quarter, profile or front). Always **measure them on
the subject, never take them from a typical breed**: the point is how this
animal differs from the average, and a ratio from a breed standard draws the
standard.

| ratio | measured as |
|---|---|
| eye spacing / head width | distance between the eye centres, over the skull's width at the eye line (cheek edge to cheek edge, or to where the far ear joins it; ear flaps and loose fur left out) |
| eye line to nose tip / head height | vertical distance from the eye line to the nose, over crown to chin |
| muzzle length / skull length | stop to nose tip, over stop to the back of the skull (in profile); in a front or three-quarter view, eye line to nose over crown to eye line |
| ear base position | where the ear's front edge leaves the skull, as a fraction of head height down from the crown and of head width from the near side |
| head width / chest width | skull width at the eye line, over the chest's widest point (in a lying or sitting pose, its width just below the jaw) |

Drawn dogs often have a jaw that is too deep or too square. When the head looks
boxy, also measure the depth from the bottom of the nose to the chin over head
height.

To read the ratios on the drawing, use the same landmarks:

- `--scan` on the head's box, from the side the face turns toward, prints the
  silhouette edge row by row for subject and drawing. This gives the skull's
  width at the eye line and the jaw's taper.
- `--overlay --box` on the head shows the two inks over each other: eye
  centres, the nose, the stop and the mouth line. For each subject line, say
  which drawn line is meant to match it and how far off it runs.
- The eye and nose centres are the dark blobs in each box. Their positions can
  be right while the skull around them is too wide, which changes every ratio
  divided by head width. That is the usual cause of a head that looks boxy with
  every feature in its place.

## A herd is individuals, and the check is arithmetic

Several animals drawn the same way are one animal stamped repeatedly, which the
rule against twinning forbids. **Write down each animal's turn before hanging
anything on it, and read the turn from the subject by where the muzzle sits
inside its own head.** Record it as a pixel offset per animal (for example
*head centre 691, muzzle centre 673, eighteen left*), so the turn is data, not
an impression.

Two neighbours facing the same way is not twinning; a real herd often has two in
a row turned in the same direction. What matters is that **no two neighbours
have the same *amount* of turn**, and that tilts are mixed in with the turns
(one animal tipped back and away, one tipped down), so each muzzle tapers
differently. Let about one animal in six look straight out.

Depth is the other axis, and the one most often drawn backwards: the smallest,
furthest animal ends up with the heaviest line in the picture. **An animal one
plane back takes a lighter weight and a duller value than one in front. When
choosing weights, settle depth before light.**

## The sub-forms an inventory descends to

An entry that stops at "the cow" or "the dog" gives the later checks only one
silhouette to judge. Write an entry for each of these that the subject shows,
and name the hidden ones in `_absent`:

```checklist
object: quadruped|animal|dog|puppy|cat|kitten|horse|pony|donkey|cow|bull|calf|ox|sheep|goat|pig|deer|fox|wolf|lion|tiger|bear
sub-forms: head ear eye muzzle|snout|nose neck body|barrel|torso leg foot|feet|hoof|hooves|paw tail
```

`check.py --checklist parts.json` reads this block and fails on any sub-form
the inventory neither names nor excuses in `_absent`.

## Build order

1. **Write down the posture** (plantigrade, digitigrade or unguligrade) and check
   it by measuring the mid-leg joint's height against the leg's length. A wrong
   posture puts every joint on the animal in the wrong place.
2. **Draw the barrel** at a measured depth against a measured length. It is the
   largest form, and everything else is placed against it.
3. **Hang the pelvis and the shoulder mass on the barrel**: hooks and pins
   behind, the ridge of the blades in front. Measure both as landmarks; neither
   can be worked out from theory.
4. **Draw the head's box, the neck's cylinder and the chest** where the two
   meet, all at the same stage, never the chest later. This is the junction
   most often lost.
5. **Draw the legs as zigzags between measured joints**, with elbow and stifle
   *inside* the body outline where the subject puts them, and hock and carpus at
   their measured mid-leg heights.
6. **Write down the turn of each head**, and place the centre line where the
   turn puts it, not down the middle of the box.
7. Draw horns, ears, eye slot and muzzle box last, each with a measured base and
   none of them a sliver.

Draw the animal **whole, keeping every part at the same stage**. A finished head
over a blocked-in chest is not an animal in progress. Walking the inventory
catches it: refuse to pass when one part is a stage ahead of another.
