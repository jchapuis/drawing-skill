# The quadruped as a solid

A herd of six cows was drawn from this skill, and what came back was six heads.
The `--masses` gate put it in four words — the subject holds *one connected
animal, poll to hoof*, and the drawing held *a head, a detached slab, and
floating legs.* The heads themselves were then rebuilt once, and after the
rebuild the worst box in the entire seventy-four-part inventory, measured by
structure rather than by ink, was still **a cow's chest** — and so was the third.

That is the shape of the failure this document exists for, and it is not about
cows. An animal is drawn as a portrait with scenery attached, because the head
is the only part the drawer has a construction for. Everything from the neck
back gets outlined, and outlining is the cardinal error.

## The schema is a diagnostic, never a scaffold

Everything below is knowledge about the **average** animal, and the average
animal is not the one in front of you. **Do not build on it.** Build on
landmarks measured off the subject, and use what follows only afterwards, to ask
why your measured animal differs from the average and whether the difference is
the breed, the pose, the individual — or a mistake.

This warning is not decorative. The most expensive error this skill has made was
a tool that handed over a *generic* form for free at exactly the stage whose job
is to find the particular one: a head built on a computed ball, with every
feature then measured honestly and hung on it, came out with every box correct
and was not a face. A barrel, a shoulder wedge and four zigzag legs are the same
trap wearing a hide. If a passage below could be followed with the subject
covered up, you are using it wrongly.

**Provenance is marked throughout.** *Bought* means a drawing paid for the claim
and the measurement is given. *Read* means it comes from the instruction
literature, is named to its source, and **has not been tested here.**

## The eight ways a drawn quadruped fails

Every one of these passes `--masses`, `--parts` and the silhouette on the round
before it is caught, so each is given with the measurement that does catch it.

| The fault | The measurement that finds it |
|---|---|
| **The animals have no bodies** — solidly built heads sitting over pale open ground | `--masses`: the subject reads as *one connected animal, poll to hoof*; the drawing as *a head, a detached slab, and floating legs* |
| **The chest is where it breaks** — not the legs, not the barrel, but the passage from neck to foreleg | Structure share in the chest box drops to around **0.44–0.57 of the subject's**, and those come out the worst boxes in the whole inventory |
| **Legs that never close into legs** — contours below the brisket crossing each other, so the pale runs on down to the ground undivided | The subject shows a leg, a gap, and a leg. Measure the gap |
| **The heads are eggs** — domed across the poll, bulging widest in the middle | The subject's is a **shield**: broad and nearly flat across the poll, then two long near-straight runs falling inward to a narrow jaw. Its widest point is at the very top |
| **Horns as wire antennae**, often twice the subject's length, with no body | They are cones — measure the width at the poll against the height they rise clear of it; the ratio is closer to 1:1.3 than to a sliver |
| **Ears as lobes** | They are **leaves that come to a point** at the outer end |
| **A blaze painted as the whole face**, the dark side panels reaching only a third of the way in | A blaze is a strip down the middle; the dark comes **most** of the way in, or each face reads as a white head with two smudges |
| **The furthest animal carries the densest line** | Sight the ink share of a far animal against a near one. It should be lighter, and the drawing routinely has it heavier |

An outline can be right while the order of widths inside it is wrong, and no
mechanical check sees that. The instrument that does is a blind describer asked
the three questions **of the head crop alone**.

## The animal is one mass before it is any parts [bought]

The finding above is the whole document compressed. Head, neck, chest, barrel
and legs are **one continuous solid** that the light crosses and the contour
runs around, and the place it is lost is always the same: the **chest**, where
the neck's cylinder turns into the ribcage and the forelegs hang off it.

That junction is hard because nothing in it is a shape you can name. The head is
a form, the leg is a column, and between them is a passage that only reads if
you have decided how the neck *enters* the chest and where the point of shoulder
juts out of it. Skip the decision and you draw the head, draw the legs, and
leave the gap open — which is exactly the 0.44.

So: **block the barrel and the chest at the same stage as the head, and check
them together.** Not the head first. A cow's head at contour over a chest at
block-in is a failed gate under the skill's own rule that no part may be more
than one stage ahead of any other.

## The skeleton under the hide [read: Ellenberger & Baum; Bammes]

Ellenberger and Baum's *Atlas of Animal Anatomy for Artists* exists because the
surface contour of an animal is bone showing through — the withers ridge, the
hip's sharp angle, the shoulder blade's edge. Bammes teaches the same animals as
**basic forms** first. Neither has been tested here; both say the following.

- **The ribcage is a barrel**, and it is the same egg you draw for a human
  thorax, rotated to lie horizontal. It is the animal's largest single form and
  it is deep rather than round — a cow's is deep and slab-sided, a sighthound's
  is deep and narrow. **Measure its depth against its length** before you draw
  a line of it.
- **There is no clavicle.** The shoulder blade is not bolted to anything: it
  **floats in a muscular sling against the outside of the ribcage** and slides
  along it as the animal moves. The whole front end hangs between the two blades
  the way a bucket hangs in a rope. Two consequences for drawing: the withers
  ridge is the blades' upper edges standing proud of the spine, and the front leg
  has no fixed pivot — its top slides, so a foreleg's angle at the shoulder
  cannot be reasoned out, only observed.
- **The pelvis is structurally the same as yours**, tilted to lie along a
  horizontal spine. On cattle its two landmarks are visible through the hide and
  are the fixed points you hang the hindquarters on: the **hooks** (the outer hip
  bones, standing out at the top of the flank) and the **pins** (the rump's rear
  corners, slightly lower). Livestock judging reads a cow off exactly these.
- **The head extends forward from the neck rather than balancing on top of it.**
  The neck is a cylinder leaving the chest at an angle, not a stalk rising from
  shoulders.

## The leg, which is where the drawing gets it backwards [read: Laws; vet anatomy]

**The joint halfway down the hind leg that looks like a knee bending the wrong
way is the hock, and the hock is an ankle.** It is the anatomical homologue of
your heel — a heel permanently held off the ground. Nothing on the animal bends
backwards. This is stated everywhere in the veterinary and naturalist literature
and is the single most repeated error artists make with quadrupeds.

The true knee — the **stifle** — is up inside the flank, level with the belly
line, usually inside the body outline entirely. The same is true in front: the
**elbow** sits down against the bottom of the ribcage, and the joint you can see
halfway down the front leg is the **carpus**, the wrist.

| What you see | What it actually is | Where the real joint is |
|---|---|---|
| "knee", front leg, mid-height | **carpus** — the wrist | The **elbow** is against the lower ribcage, often inside the silhouette |
| "backwards knee", hind leg | **hock** — the ankle/heel | The **stifle** (the knee) is up in the flank at belly level |
| The whole lower leg | **hand and foot bones**, hugely elongated — the cannon is one fused metacarpal/metatarsal | — |
| The "ankle" just above the hoof | the **fetlock** — a knuckle | — |
| The hoof | a **toenail**, and the animal stands on its tip | — |

John Muir Laws' comparative-leg sheet gives the practical test, and it is a
measurement rather than a guess: **on an unguligrade animal the wrist sits
halfway between the elbow and the hoof**, and the heel sits midway along the
visible hind leg. Plantigrade animals (bears, humans) put the whole sole down and
the wrist reaches the ground; digitigrade ones (dogs, cats) carry the wrist just
above it. So measure the height of the mid-leg joint against the leg's total
length and the posture falls out — do not infer the posture from the species and
then place the joint to match.

What this buys you at the drawing board: the hind leg is a **zigzag of four
runs** — a short hidden femur going down-and-forward, a long tibia going
down-and-back to the hock, the cannon dropping vertically, the pastern angling
forward to the hoof. The drive comes from the top of the zigzag, inside the body,
which is why a hind leg reads as powerful only when the flank mass above it is
drawn as part of the leg.

## The head [bought, except where marked]

A bovine head is a **muzzle box** hung on a **cranial mass**, and both are
squarer than you will draw them. The error is reliable enough to predict: you
will draw an **egg** — domed across the top, widest in the middle — where the
subject has a **shield**: broad and near-flat across the poll, then two long
**near-straight runs** falling inward to a narrow jaw. That is a wedge with a
flat top, and its widest point is at the very top, not in the middle.

The bony reason, from the veterinary literature and untested here: cattle have
**wide, robust frontal bones** that form the flat plate of the poll, with the
orbits set out at the sides of it rather than facing forward, and the **cornual
processes projecting from the frontal bone near its rear edge**. So the horns
grow out of the corners of a flat plate — which is what fixes their spacing and
why they cannot be moved inward to make a nicer silhouette.

Measured off the subject, and every one of these was wrong before it was
measured:

- **Horns are cones**, about **14px across where they leave the poll** and
  rising about **18 clear of it** at that panel's scale. Drawn as slivers they
  came back as wire antennae twice the subject's length. A horn has a base.
- **Ears are leaves that come to a point** at the outer end, set below and
  outside the horns. Drawn as lobes they read as loops.
- **The eye is a white slot about 16 across by 6 deep**, cut into the dark of the
  face, with a pupil about 6 wide sitting centred and touching the upper lid.
  Sixteen by six on every one of the six animals — a heavy-lidded letterbox, not
  an almond.
- **The muzzle pads measured 55, 46, 46 and 48 wide** across four animals, and
  the remaining fault after the rebuild was not their size but that the subject
  leaves **more white cheek either side of them**, so its blaze wins the head and
  ours ties with the muzzle.
- **The blaze is a strip down the middle.** The dark side panels must come
  **most** of the way in; at a third of the way in each face reads as a white
  head with two smudges.

## A herd is individuals, and the check is arithmetic [bought]

Six animals drawn the same way is one animal stamped six times, which is exactly
what principle 7 forbids. The device that stopped it was cheap: **write down each
animal's turn before hanging anything on it, and read the turn off the subject by
where the muzzle sits inside its own head.**

Record it as an offset in pixels, per animal — *head centre 691, muzzle centre
673, eighteen left* — so the turn is data and not an impression.

What stops a herd twinning is **not** that no two neighbours face the same way;
a real herd will happily give you two in a row turned the same direction. What
holds is that **no two neighbours have the same *amount* of turn**, and that the
tips are mixed in with the yaws — one animal tipped back and away, one tipped
down — so that six tapers are six different tapers. Let about one animal in six
look straight out.

Depth is the other axis and it is the one drawn backwards most often: the
smallest, furthest animal ends up carrying the heaviest line in the panel. **An
animal one plane back takes a lighter weight and a duller value than one in
front, and the weight ladder is spent on depth before it is spent on light.**

## Build order

1. **Say the posture in writing** — plantigrade, digitigrade or unguligrade — and
   check it by measuring the mid-leg joint's height against the leg's length.
   Getting this wrong puts every joint on the animal in the wrong place.
2. **The barrel**, as a measured depth against a measured length. It is the
   largest form and everything else is placed against it.
3. **The pelvis and the shoulder mass**, hung on the barrel — hooks and pins
   behind, the blade's ridge in front. Neither is a joint you may reason out; both
   are landmarks you measure.
4. **The head's box and the neck's cylinder**, and the **chest** where the two
   meet — at the same stage, never after. This is the junction the drawing loses.
5. **The legs as zigzags between measured joints**, elbow and stifle placed
   *inside* the body outline where the subject puts them, hock and carpus at their
   measured mid-leg heights.
6. **The turn of each head, written down**, and the centre line placed where the
   turn puts it rather than down the middle of the box.
7. Horns, ears, eye slot and muzzle box last — each with a measured base, none of
   them a sliver.

The animal is drawn **whole and at one stage throughout**. A finished head over a
blocked-in chest is not an animal in progress; it is the drawing that already
failed here once, and the gate that catches it is walking the inventory and
refusing to pass when one part is a stage ahead of another.
