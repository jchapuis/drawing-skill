# Drawing a scene

A scene is a different problem from a bigger subject. A single subject of about
twenty named parts comes out coherent. A scene of seventy or eighty parts comes
out with every part thin: a garment that is a balloon, a hip with no joint in
it, a tyre that never closes. None of these is a mistake in itself, and more
marks per part only make the picture busier.

The cause is that nothing organised the picture above the level of the object
[read: source]. Loomis: *"More pictures are bad because no attempt at tone
organization has been made than for any other reason"*, and he names the symptom:
*"tones so scattered and broken up as not to have the much-needed impact"*. A
picture of eighty equally shallow parts fails for lack of a dominant tone and a
single centre of interest, not for lack of work.

So design the picture as one thing before you draw it as many.

One thing does change with the number of parts: how much you can see of each.
A render is viewed at a fixed size, so a feature that fills 10% of a
single-object crop is 1-3% of the whole picture, too small to judge as a shape.
So look at every focus object magnified (`--zoom`) and draw it at the working
resolution its smallest feature needs. This is separate from the design: an
object drawn well inside an undesigned picture still breaks the picture.

## The mass is the unit, and objects are named afterwards [read: source]

Painting traditions all arrive at the same order of work.

Loomis, on where to start: *"It is better, actually, to think of the setting
before you think of the figure."* And on the working procedure: *"make some
patterns of dark (eventually rocks), some greys (eventually shadow or sky), and
some whites (eventually clouds). We soon find the spot for the bathing girl."*
A dark shape is placed first and called a rock later.

Speed: *"The reducing of a complicated appearance to a few simple masses is
the first necessity of the painter"*, and his block-in is explicitly *"not really
a drawing at all, but a species of mapping out"*. Carolus-Duran's atelier taught
that a painter *"does not consider it advisable to finish each bit at a time,
but prefers that all the tones should first be blocked in very coarsely"*.
Gurney gives it as an instruction, after Pyle: merge adjacent tonal shapes into
larger abstract patterns. *"Put your white against white, middle tones against
grays, black against black, then black and white where you want your center of
interest."*

Loomis also names what goes wrong in the opposite order: finishing a figure and
then *"trying to fill the spaces left around it"* with background *"usually
results in [poor] arrangement or none at all."*

### When does an object get to exist?

Two things decide it:

- **Distance from the one centre of interest.** Guptill: *"the strongest
  contrasts and sharpest details appear at this center of interest, and grow
  less and less distinct towards the edges of the paper"*, and *"be careful not
  to have two or more centers showing equal emphasis."*
- **Contrast with its own surround.** An object gets a contour only if it
  differs in value from the mass it sits in. Otherwise it stays inside the mass
  and is never drawn as a separate thing. Loomis: *"surface detail should become
  submerged in tone as it goes back… the distance can become almost poster in
  effect."*

## Most edges are lost, and edges belong to the mass stage

Decide edges at the mass stage, not the contour stage. This is where scenes have
actually failed, and the literature treats edges as central.

Loomis: *"Contour cannot be continuously defined all around all units and a
sense of space be achieved… If the edge is kept hard all around, it cannot avoid
sticking to the picture plane"*, and *"perhaps the most important element in
obtaining freedom and looseness is found in the treatment of edges."* Speed
observes lost and found edges at the mass stage, before any contour exists. It
follows from Pyle's rule that most junctions in a scene should be lost.

Running the object stages on each object separately does the opposite: every
object gets its own closed contour, so every junction is two found edges at
equal weight, with no occlusion accent and no stage that looked at the junction
itself. Neighbouring objects then meet as two complete outlines that cross. In
one test, the stray-looking marks across a group of objects were the second
copy of every shared edge, drawn once for each neighbour from two different
guesses.

Two facts about edges [read: source]. The sharpest shadow is where one surface
touches another, so contact points carry the darkest accent in the picture, and
that accent is what makes a thing sit on the ground. Two edges that only graze
each other (a tangent) make the space ambiguous. Fix a tangent with a decisive
overlap or a clear separation, never a near miss.

Losing an edge is also the only cheap way to push a competing object back. A
translucent glaze over it only makes a dirty copy in place. To make an object
recede, remove its contour and let it merge into the mass behind it.

## The budget is a design decision, not a division

Tiers are masses, not ranked objects. Which tier something belongs to is
decided by its contrast with its surround and its distance from the one centre
of interest, not by how important it is. The furthest tier gets no marks of its
own: it lives inside a mass and receives at most one found edge.

Do not aim for "the fewest confident marks" per object: few marks still give
each object a closed contour, which is what breaks the picture. Aim for no
silhouette instead.

A middle tier ("stated": only the edges that differ, drawn at the picture's
scale) was tried and failed. In one test every object given that treatment came
out crude, furniture and figure alike: a lamp became a diamond on a stick, a
strip of tape four floating squares, a torso one bean with no shoulder, while
an object drawn at four times the picture's scale came out well. At the
picture's scale you cannot see well enough to draw, whatever the economy of
marks. So there are two tiers:

| tier | what it is | treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **part** | Every object that must be recognisable (the figure, the vehicle, the lamp, the picture on the wall), each an object and never a feature of one | The single-subject stages, in the picture's one document at the working resolution its smallest feature needs, looked at with `--zoom`. Emphasis is the line weight and edge count it is drawn with, decided at stage 0. A far part is drawn lighter and sparer from the start, not thinned afterwards |
| **mass** | Everything else, which is most of the list | Inside a mass. No contour and no marks of its own, except in a source that inks every flat boundary, where the mass keeps its one line (`redrawing.md`) |

There is no shortcut on cost: a figure or a vehicle is a whole single-subject
run, and a lamp is a tenth of one. Each named object is either paid for or
merged into a mass, and the reading decides which before any part is begun.

### Named devices for what is not rendered [read: source]

Every tradition has techniques for the fact that you cannot render everything:

- **Tonal plan / notan**: one dominant tone and two to four value masses for the
  whole picture, decided before anything is drawn.
- **Shape welding** (Gurney's term): adjacent objects of the same value fused
  into one shape, with objects lost in shadow.
- **Finish gradient from one centre.** Guptill: *"start at the center of
  interest and work out from there, continuing the sketch only until the drawing
  includes enough area and is sufficiently rendered to properly convey its
  message."*
- **Indication**: half an outline still reads. *"To suggest or indicate brings
  more interesting and more artistic results… than does tiresomely complete
  outline."*
- **Texture fields.** Foliage, crowds and brick are a pattern studied once and
  applied as a field, never listed object by object. A field the describer names
  (a rock field, a crowd) is still a part, because the acceptance list will ask
  for it, but draw it this way: its sub-forms are the unit types plus the units
  at the boundary nearest the focus, which get full treatment, and the rest are
  flats with a silhouette and no interior line. On a flat-cel source that
  inks every boundary, record the far units' missing interior ink as a
  departure; it does not break the rule. A faceted field (rocks, crystals,
  folded paper) is straights throughout, so set `smooth=False` on every stroke:
  rendered smooth, a rock field became river stones and every gate still passed.
  Inking every face's closed outline draws each shared edge twice (`--doubled`
  went from 20 to 29 on one field). Ink a unit's silhouette once and its
  interior face edges as open strokes, writing each shared edge once from the
  point dict.
- **Distance to poster**: fewer planes, less halftone and flatter light the
  further back, with line weight thinning with depth.
- **Vignette**: the drawing fades into the paper, and the silhouette the whole
  drawing makes against the paper is itself a designed shape.
- **Two treatments on purpose**: the comics' masking effect, with iconic figures
  against a realistic setting, deliberately left unreconciled.

### Where the traditions disagree

Comics, bande dessinée and manga do work object by object, by different hands. A
mangaka draws the characters while assistants do the backgrounds, and Studios
Hergé had one artist for décors and another for vehicles. Animation goes
furthest: characters and backgrounds are different artists in different media.

That is labour divided inside a scene-level design. A layout fixes perspective and placement before anyone draws, the two
treatments are chosen on purpose, and a late unifying pass spots the blacks
across the whole image. Nobody starts from eighty-four parts.

## Overlaps are objects, and they are where scenes fail

An overlap is a place where two forms touch or cross. Typical failures:

| overlap | how it fails |
|---|---|
| hand / handle | one fused dark mass, where the subject separates them with a band of its own ground or a second lighter value |
| foot / pedal | two discs, until a sole and a strap slot go in |
| hip / seat | no joint at all, with supports leaving the bottom of a black shape |
| limb / tube behind it | the tube's ink runs straight over the limb in front |
| wheel / floor | the ring never closes, so nothing bears weight |
| shoulder / arm | no upper arm; the forearm starts at the garment's edge |

- **Put the overlaps in `parts.json` as named parts with their own boxes.** No
  check looks at the gap between two boxes unless it is named.
- **Draw the overlap at the same time as both objects it joins.** A hand drawn
  onto a finished bar looks stuck on.
- **Occlusion is a matter of order.** Marks render in the order they are
  written, and a flat is opaque, so a nearer group's fills laid after a farther
  group's ink cover it exactly where the near form is. Write each depth group as
  its fills then its ink, back to front. Do not send every fill to the back of
  the scene: then no flat can hide ink, and every far contour has to be cut by
  hand. What remains to draw at the crossing is the accent where the two forms
  touch.
- **Decide the depth order for each crossing in writing at stage 0**, in an
  `in front` column beside the overlap. No mark records it (a stroke has no z,
  and the checks cannot ask). Without it, the order is settled by whichever mark
  happened to be written last, which is how a chainring ends up drawn across the
  shoe standing on it.
- **One edge, one stroke.** Where two objects share a boundary, draw it once, as
  one object's, and stop the other object's contour at it. An edge drawn twice
  from two guesses is the commonest mark-level fault in a scene.

If you skip the depth order, two objects that cross come out as one black mass.
The cause is usually not value (both are often the same colour in the subject)
but that the far object's marks were drawn over the near one. Once the order is
decided, writing the marks in that order fixes it exactly. `check.py --depth` reads
it. Never judge a crossing at 1:1, where one a few pixels wrong looks identical
to one that is right.

## Studies, and how they reduce the budget [read: comics and atelier practice]

A scene cannot afford to build eighty parts. The profession's answer is the
study, in a standard pipeline: thumbnails settle the composition before anything
is drawn at size, elements are worked on a separate sheet and traced through,
and roughs are printed as bluelines on the final sheet and drawn over, never
redrawn from memory. The oldest version is the Renaissance *cartone*, worked
until it is right, then pricked and pounced onto the wall.

It works because an object drawn on its own is a small subject again, and gets
a small subject's attention. Here, though, keep the study and the magnified look
and drop the transfer: objects drawn in their own crop and composited back came
out as blobs, while the same objects drawn in place came out well.

### A study builds the capacity to draw the thing, not one copy of it

A study is the same form drawn several times, off the picture, from different
angles, until you understand it as a solid. Only then draw the picture's
instance, from that understanding and not from the reference's outline.

Drawn once, in place, at the angle the picture needs, it is a traced silhouette,
which you cannot check or correct (`method.md`, principle 5). The test is
mechanical: if you cannot draw the object at an angle the reference does
not show, you have copied it, not studied it.

### Where a study lives, and how the picture gets it

A study goes in its own document, because it is practice. Draw the picture's
instance in the picture's document, in place and in its coordinates, from what
the study taught you. Nothing is transferred, and marks share one coordinate
space. Measuring can still be done per object: `crop.py` cuts an object's crop out of the working-resolution subject at
1:1 and stores its corner in the PNG, so its `trace.py` regions come back in the
picture's coordinates with no flag. `--zoom` gives the magnified look.

```bash
python3 crop.py subject.png 200,560,460,540 meas/bike.png                # corner stored; trace.py reads it
node harness/cli.mjs DOC.json --only bike,frame --png bike.png          # the object alone, in the picture
```

`stroke` takes `tag=` for the object and for the edge, and `stage=` for the
stage, and `--only` / `--hide` match tags.

Order depth per tube, not per object. "Draw the bike, then the figure" is too
coarse: the top tube passes in front of the far leg and the seat tube passes
behind the near one. An object is not at one depth, so write it in tagged depth
groups, each interleaved with the other objects' groups.

In one document both objects of an overlap are always present. Draw each
overlap as the second of its two objects goes in, and give overlaps the budget
the tiers saved.

Only the part at the centre of interest gets the 4x detail pass at its own stage
10. The rest pass their own gates and stop.

## Splitting a scene across agents

Divide a picture by object, not by area. Two agents each taking half a picture
meet at a seam nobody owns. One agent per focus object meets the others at the
overlaps, which the picture has to name anyway.

Studying and painting can be done by different agents; reading and drawing
cannot. The agent that paints must look at the subject itself, and a handoff
only directs that looking. Treat it as a hypothesis from a usually reliable
source: a wrong statement in it is caught only when the painter re-measures.
That includes an asymmetry stated backwards, which inverts a likeness while
every box stays correct.

**What transfers**

- **Decisions**, which are expensive to reach and cheap to state: the light
  (including *there is none*, which is a measurement and not an omission), the
  value groups and the empty gap between them, the depth order at every
  junction, and what to leave out.
- **Numbers**: landmarks, the measured weight span, the palette with where each
  value was sampled, and how this subject differs from the typical one, with the
  direction each feature is to be pushed. Say also which features are canonical
  (match the typical form) and must not be pushed.
- **The count** (below).

**What does not transfer**

- **Boxes.** A bounding box cannot become a mark, and a box must never be drawn
  to. Hand over measured contours (the runs of points), or expect the outline to
  be measured twice.
- **Procedures.** A handoff passage that could be followed with the subject
  covered up is a recipe, and it arrives at the stage whose job is to find the
  particular form.
- **A weight swatch as numbers.** Each instrument has its own weights and
  nothing in the numbers says so. The agent that paints draws and measures its
  own swatch.

### The mechanics: sections, copies, merge

Splitting by part gives every part full attention. In one test, one agent that
rationed its effort left every part but the focus at its S2 mass. The same scene
drawn by one agent per part matched the subject's dark area at 0.80 against
0.63, and a blind viewer named every part. The procedure:

1. **One agent takes S0-S2 and the split.** It produces everything S0 and S2
   produce, plus:
   - `draw.py` in depth order as **sections**, each opened by
     `# === part: NAME ===` or `# === mass: NAME ===` and closed by
     `# === end: NAME ===`, each holding that part's S2 mass lines.
   - **A section is a depth group, not an object.** An object that straddles
     another in depth (a far leg behind a frame and a near leg in front; rocks
     behind a wheel's spokes and over its tyre) goes to the agent of the thing
     it is interleaved with, or gets a second section (`part: rocks.back`). In
     one test, three of six objects straddled.
   - `split.md`, for each part: its keys and tag prefix, its box, its smallest
     feature, the sections before and after it, every overlap row it is on and
     who is in front, its lost edges, its emphasis, the acceptance-list items it
     must make true, and which other agents share each one (a saddle whose only
     visible edge is the shorts' outline cannot be made right by the bicycle's
     agent alone).
   - Each S2 mass is **tagged with every sub-form it stands in for**
     (`leg.near.thigh+leg.near.knee+leg.near.shin`). Otherwise rows written for
     the crossing forms' sub-forms have nothing to resolve against.
   - **Masses are finished here, line and texture included**, because no later
     agent owns them. A mass's crest runs under the nearest part's crest ink,
     never above it: a part's agent cannot paint bare ground back, so a rock bed
     that overshoots its pile stays wrong until the final pass. A mass may be several polygons (a far rock seen through
     spokes can be an island). A texture that stands on a mass's silhouette
     (grass tufts above a band's edge) is part of its outline, whatever the
     describer calls it.
2. **Each part's agent works in its own copy of the directory** and edits only
   between its own markers; a fix to a neighbour's section goes in its gap log
   for the final pass. The part runs stages 2-10 with its own rounds. What it
   measures on its own zoom (a count, a number of spokes) overrides S0's
   numbers, and it writes the correction into its entry. It may add rows to
   `parts.json` for its own sub-forms. It sets no round cap and inherits none: a
   cap written into `reading.md` at S0 can stop a part's agent midway.
3. **Merge.** Replace each section of the master `draw.py` with the part's own,
   after checking that each copy changed only its own section. Merge
   `parts.json` entry by entry, not as text (an agent may rewrite the whole
   file's whitespace), and flag any key two agents changed differently. A
   palette name one agent added is merged as is; one an agent re-sampled is
   merged only when no other section draws with it. A mark that a part's agent
   found must sit in front of a later section (a hub nut in front of the fork)
   moves to a small section of its own after that one.
4. **Judge each part again on the merged picture.** While a part was drawn its
   crop showed neighbours still at S2, so any judgement in a copy that it was
   finished is provisional, and a critic shown those crops reports the
   stand-ins as the part's faults. `--ranking`, `--counts` and `--parts` read
   mid-split are not verdicts. Show a part's critic `--only`/`--hide` renders of
   the part alone.
5. **One final agent takes S4-S8 on the merged picture**, starting from the
   part agents' notes about their neighbours. Then walk `--unfilled` in full: a
   bare triangle of jersey at the seam between two sections is the kind of
   patch nobody else sees.

### The count: the check that catches an extra part

Every other instrument asks whether what was drawn is right. The count asks how
many there are. Before drawing, state the count of every countable thing,
including the zeroes: no nostrils, no lips as shapes, no shadow on a face that
carries none. Walking the inventory catches a missing part. Nothing else catches
a part drawn twice, a third limb or a second shadow, because an extra part has
no box to be absent from.

### Accept a delegated drawing by its script, before looking at it

Run `check.py --stages ops.json` on what comes back. A drawing that measured
honestly but skipped stages 6 and 8 has its lines as filled shapes, no mark
with its own taper, and passes every placement check. This applies to lines,
the edges of forms: a small form dark all through (a droplet whose ink is wider
than its interior) is legitimately a dark flat with its interior written over
it. `--stages` also fails a drawing whose
marks were generated rather than written in its script. A script built by
concatenating generated sections is not a drawing, however well its render
passes.

### Budget, and why a self-reported one is not a budget

Studying and then painting one object to this depth can cost more than a whole
flat picture, and a scene needs one per part. That is why the part list is fixed
at stage 0, and why a scene with many recognisable objects is expensive. Enforce the budget from outside the agent, at checkpoints:
after the masses, after the first part, after the last. An agent's own estimate
of what it has spent can be low by more than a factor of two.

## Order of work

Steps 0-3 reverse the usual order: none of them draws an object.

0. **Read the source as a tone field.** Posterise it to three or four values and
   measure the masses, not the objects. Say in one sentence what the picture is
   about, and pick one centre of interest. *Done when* a thumbnail notan reads as
   a design with one dominant value and the strongest contrast at the focus.
1. **Armature.** Eye level, ground plane, the main perspective lines, the
   frame's subdivision, and the major masses' silhouettes. Still no objects.
   *Done when* every later shape can be located on this ground.
2. **Tonal plan and merging.** Give every region a value class and merge
   adjacent same-value objects into single shapes. The output is five to twelve
   shapes, not eighty-four parts. Record for each shape edge whether it is sharp
   or lost. *Done when* the shape map matches the posterised source at thumbnail
   size and no object has a closed contour.
3. **Block the masses** in straights: lay the middle tone over the whole, then
   the light shapes, then the darks, following the sharp/lost decisions. *Done
   when* the downsampled render matches step 2.
4. **Every part: run the single-subject stages** unchanged, each in place in the
   picture's document, far to near, by depth group. The part at the centre of
   interest goes to the full quality bar. The others pass their gates at the
   lighter emphasis decided in step 2 and stop.
5. **Emphasis gradient.** Decide it before the parts are drawn and confirm it
   here: each part's line weight and edge count fall with distance from the
   centre of interest and with depth, and were drawn that way, not adjusted
   afterwards. *Done when* no part beyond the focus
   carries the focus's weight and a describer still names every part.
6. **Junction pass, as its own stage.** Walk every place two things meet, decide
   lost or found, put the occlusion accent where forms touch, and break tangents
   by overlap or separation. *Done when* every junction has a recorded decision.
7. **Texture fields.** A few units drawn at the boundary nearest the focus, the
   rest as one indicated pattern.
8. **Spot the blacks and vignette.** One pass placing the black pattern across
   objects rather than within them, then a check of the whole drawing's
   silhouette against the paper.
9. **Correct from a distance.** Downsample again and compare against step 0,
   and confirm there is still exactly one focus.

Passing a stage gate means no part was skipped, not that every part had equal
work: at the masses gate a background band is finished as one shape while a
focus object has only begun, and both are at the same stage. Walk the inventory for omissions, not for equal depth.

**Budget by stage, not only by part.** On a twelve-object scene taken to the
masses gate, the split was: stage 0 (reading, measuring, the inventory and its
blind validation) 55%; gesture, envelope and block-in 15%; construction 3%;
masses plus one correction round 27%. Stage 0 on a scene is more than half the
job, and should be. There is also a fixed cost the tiers do not show: loading
this skill takes a material share of a scene's budget, and in one test both
agents spent roughly a third of their run reading before a mark existed.
