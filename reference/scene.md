# Drawing a scene

A scene is not a bigger subject. It is a different problem, and for a long time
this file said the difference was arithmetic — that quality falls off because a
fixed budget divides by the part count. **That explanation is wrong**, and it is
worth saying why, because the tier list built on top of it failed twice.

The observation behind it is real and stands [bought: drawing]: a single subject
of about twenty named parts comes out coherent, and a scene of seventy or eighty
parts arrives with **every part thin** — a garment that is a balloon, a hip with
no joint in it, a tyre that never closes. Nothing in it is a *mistake*. But the
cause is not that the marks ran out. Feed the same panel more marks per part and
it does not get better; it gets busier.

The cause is that **nothing organised the picture above the level of the
object** [read: source]. Loomis is blunt about it: *"More pictures are bad
because no attempt at tone organization has been made than for any other
reason"*, and he names the symptom exactly — *"tones so scattered and broken up
as not to have the much-needed impact"*. A picture of eighty equally shallow
parts fails for want of **a dominant tone and a single centre of interest**, not
for want of work.

So the variable you control is not how unevenly you spend. It is whether the
picture is designed as one thing before it is drawn as many.

One thing does divide, and it is not the marks: **the eye**. A render is looked
at at a fixed size, so a feature that is 10% of a single-object crop is 1–3% of
a panel, below what can be judged as a shape [bought: drawing]. That is why every
focus object is looked at magnified and drawn at a working resolution its
smallest feature needs (below), and it is a separate fact from the design one: an
object drawn well inside an undesigned panel still breaks the picture.

## The mass is the unit, and objects are named afterwards [read: source]

This is the whole reversal, and every painting tradition reached states it.

Loomis, on where to start: *"It is better, actually, to think of the setting
before you think of the figure."* And on the working procedure: *"make some
patterns of dark (eventually rocks), some greys (eventually shadow or sky), and
some whites (eventually clouds). We soon find the spot for the bathing girl."*
The rocks are not drawn and then toned. **A dark shape is placed, and it is
called a rock later.**

Speed: *"The reducing of a complicated appearance to a few simple masses is the
first necessity of the painter"* — and his block-in is explicitly *"not really a
drawing at all, but a species of mapping out"*. Carolus-Duran's atelier taught
that a painter *"does not consider it advisable to finish each bit at a time,
but prefers that all the tones should first be blocked in very coarsely"*.
Gurney's **shapewelding** puts it as an instruction: fuse adjacent tonal shapes
into larger abstract patterns, after Pyle — *"Put your white against white,
middle tones against grays, black against black, then black and white where you
want your center of interest."*

Loomis also names the failure that comes of doing it the other way: finishing a
figure and then *"trying to fill the spaces left around it"* with background
*"usually results in [poor] arrangement or none at all."*

### When does an object get to exist?

Two governors, both sourced:

- **Distance from the one centre of interest.** Guptill: *"the strongest
  contrasts and sharpest details appear at this center of interest, and grow
  less and less distinct towards the edges of the paper"* — and *"be careful not
  to have two or more centers showing equal emphasis."*
- **Contrast with its own surround.** An object earns a contour by differing in
  value from the mass it sits in. Otherwise it stays inside the mass and is never
  drawn as a thing at all. Loomis: *"surface detail should become submerged in
  tone as it goes back… the distance can become almost poster in effect."*

## Most edges are lost, and edges belong to the mass stage

Not to the contour stage. This is where our scenes have actually failed, and the
literature does not treat junctions as a side problem — **edges are the problem**.

Loomis: *"Contour cannot be continuously defined all around all units and a
sense of space be achieved… If the edge is kept hard all around, it cannot avoid
sticking to the picture plane"*, and *"perhaps the most important element in
obtaining freedom and looseness is found in the treatment of edges."* Speed
observes lost-and-foundness **at the mass stage**, before any contour exists.
Pyle's rule, read for its consequence: the **majority** of junctions in a scene
should be lost.

**What a per-object ladder does instead** [bought: drawing]: it hands every
object a closed contour of its own, so every junction is found against found at
equal weight, with no occlusion accent and no stage at which the junction itself
was the subject. Two neighbouring objects then meet as two complete outlines
that cross. Measured on one panel, the marks crossing a herd's faces were not
stray construction — they were **the second copy of every shared edge**, drawn
once as one object's silhouette and again as its neighbour's, from two different
guesses.

The physical facts that go with it [read: source]: the sharpest shadow is where
one surface touches another, so contact points carry the **darkest accent** in
the picture, and that accent is what makes a thing sit on the ground. Two edges
that merely graze — a **tangent** — read as spatial ambiguity, and the fix is
decisive overlap or clear separation, never a near miss.

**Losing an edge is also the only cheap way to knock a competing object back.**
A translucent glaze over what shouts does not push it back a plane; it makes a
dirty copy of it in place [bought: drawing]. What recedes an object is removing
its contour and letting it weld into the mass behind it.

## The budget is a design decision, not a division

The tier list is salvageable, but not as it was written.

> **Tiers are welded shapes, not ranked objects.** Membership is decided by
> contrast with the surround and by distance from the one centre of interest —
> not by semantic importance. And the furthest tier gets **no marks of its own**:
> it lives inside a mass and receives at most one found edge.

"State it in the fewest confident marks" was the error [bought: drawing]. Few
marks per object still yields **a closed contour per object**, which is the thing
that breaks the picture. The instruction is not *fewer marks*; it is *no
silhouette*.

A middle tier — "stated": only the edges that differ, drawn at panel scale —
was tried and is refuted [bought: drawing]. On one panel every object given
that treatment came out crude, the furniture as much as the figure's body: a
lamp became a diamond on a stick, a strip of tape four floating squares, a
torso one bean with no shoulder, while the one object drawn magnified, at four
times the panel's scale, came out well. The eye that draws at panel scale
is the eye that cannot see at panel scale, and no economy of marks changes
that. So there are two tiers:

| tier | what it is | treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **part** | Every object that must be recognisable — the figure, the vehicle, the lamp, the picture on the wall — each an *object*, never a feature of one | The single-subject ladder, in the panel's one document at the working resolution its smallest feature needs, looked at with `--zoom`. Emphasis is the weight pitch and the edge count it is drawn with, decided at stage 0; a far part is drawn lighter and sparer, not thinned afterwards |
| **welded** | Everything else, and it is most of the list | Inside a mass. No contour, no marks of its own — except in a source that inks every flat boundary, where the mass keeps its one line (`redrawing.md`) |

The cost is real and has no shortcut: a figure or a vehicle is a whole
single-subject run; a lamp is a tenth of one. A named object is either paid
for or welded, and the reading decides which before any part is begun.

### Named devices for what is not rendered [read: source]

Every tradition has an answer to *you cannot render everything*, and they are
specific techniques, not "draw the important bit more":

- **Tonal plan / notan** — one dominant tone, two to four value masses for the
  whole picture, decided before anything is drawn.
- **Shapewelding** — adjacent same-value objects fused into one shape; objects
  lost in shadow.
- **Finish gradient from one centre** — Guptill: *"start at the center of
  interest and work out from there, continuing the sketch only until the drawing
  includes enough area and is sufficiently rendered to properly convey its
  message."*
- **Indication** — half an outline still reads. *"To suggest or indicate brings
  more interesting and more artistic results… than does tiresomely complete
  outline."*
- **Texture fields** — foliage, crowds, brick are a *pattern* studied once and
  applied as a field, never enumerated as objects. A field the describer names
  (a rock field, a crowd) is still a part — the acceptance list will ask for
  it — and it is drawn with this device: its sub-form list is the unit types
  and the units at the boundary nearest the focus, which get full treatment;
  the rest are flats with a silhouette and no interior line. On a flat-cel
  source that inks every boundary, the far units' missing interior ink is a
  departure to write down, not a rule broken. A **faceted** field — rocks,
  crystals, folded paper — is straights throughout (`smooth=False` on every
  stroke: rendered smooth, one field became river stones and every gate
  passed), and inking every face's closed outline states each shared edge
  twice (`--doubled` went from 20 to 29 on one field). Ink a unit's silhouette
  once and its interior face edges as open strokes, each shared edge written
  once from the point dict.
- **Distance to poster** — fewer planes, less halftone, flatter light the further
  back; line weight thinning with depth.
- **Vignette** — the drawing fades into the paper, and the silhouette the whole
  drawing makes against the paper is itself a designed shape.
- **Two treatments on purpose** — comics' *masking effect*: iconic figures
  against a realistic setting, deliberately not reconciled.

### Where the traditions disagree

Comics, bande dessinée and manga **do** work object by object, by different
hands — a mangaka draws characters while assistants do backgrounds; Studios
Hergé had one artist for décors and another for vehicles. Animation is the
extreme: characters and backgrounds are different artists in different media.

But that is labour division inside a scene-level design, not a counterexample. A
**layout** fixes perspective and placement before anyone draws; the two
treatments are chosen deliberately; and a late unifying pass spots the blacks
across the whole panel. Nobody starts from eighty-four parts.

## Interfaces are objects, and they are where scenes fail

| interface | how it fails |
|---|---|
| hand / handle | one fused dark mass, where the subject separates them with a band of its own ground or a second lighter value |
| foot / pedal | two discs, until a sole and a strap slot go in |
| hip / seat | no joint at all — supports leaving the bottom of a black shape |
| limb / tube behind it | the tube's ink runs straight over the limb in front |
| wheel / floor | the ring never closes, so nothing bears weight |
| shoulder / arm | no upper arm; the forearm starts at the garment's edge |

- **Put the interfaces in `parts.json` as named parts with their own boxes.** They
  are exactly the gap between two boxes, and no check looks there unless named.
- **Draw the interface at the same time as both objects it joins.** A hand drawn
  onto a finished bar is a hand stuck on.
- **Occlusion is order.** Marks render in the order they are written, and a
  flat is opaque, so a nearer group's fills laid *after* a farther group's ink
  cover it exactly where the near form is. Write each depth group as its fills
  then its ink, back to front, and never send every fill to the back of the
  scene — under that rule a flat cannot hide ink and every far
  contour has to be cut by hand. What remains to draw at the crossing is the
  accent where the two forms touch.
- **Decide the depth order per crossing in writing, at stage 0**, in an `in front`
  column beside the interface itself. No mark records it: a stroke has no z, and
  the checks have no way to ask. Without it the order is settled by whichever
  mark happened to be written last, which is how a chainring came to be drawn
  across the shoe standing on it.
- **One edge, one stroke.** Where two objects share a boundary, that boundary is
  drawn **once**, as one object's, and the other object's contour stops at it. An
  edge drawn twice from two guesses is the commonest mark-level fault in every
  scene here [bought: drawing].

**What it costs when depth order is skipped.** Two objects that cross come out as
one black mass, and the cause is usually not value — measure both and they are
often the same colour in the subject too. The cause is that the far object's
marks were drawn over the near one and nothing had decided they were behind it.
Once the decision exists the fix is the order the marks are written in, and it
is exact by construction; `check.py --depth` reads it. **Never judge a crossing at 1:1** — one that is a few pixels
wrong and one that is right look identical there.

## Studies, and how they buy back the budget [read: comics and atelier practice]

A scene cannot afford to build eighty parts. Studies are how the profession has
always got round it, and they are the standard pipeline: thumbnails settle the
composition before anything is drawn at size; elements are worked on a separate
sheet and traced through; roughs are printed as **bluelines** on the final sheet
and drawn over, never redrawn from memory. The oldest version is the Renaissance
*cartone*, worked until it is right, then pricked and pounced onto the wall.

The reason it works is the arithmetic that *does* hold: **an object drawn on its
own is a small subject again**, and gets a small subject's attention however many
parts the panel has. The transfer step is the part that does not survive here:
objects drawn in their own crop and composited back came out as blobs, and the
same objects drawn in place came out well [bought: drawing]. What survives is
the study and the magnified look.

### A study builds the capacity to draw the thing, not one copy of it

A study is **the same form drawn several times, off the panel, from different
angles** — until the form is understood as a solid. Only then is the panel's
instance drawn, from that understanding rather than from the reference's outline.

Draw it once, in place, at the angle the panel needs, and you have a traced
silhouette: it records the 2D boundary from one angle, contradicts nothing, and
therefore has no way to be wrong — which is the same as no way to be corrected.
The test is mechanical: **if you cannot draw the object at an angle the reference
does not show, you have not studied it — you have copied it.**

### Where a study lives, and how the panel gets it

A study goes in **its own document** — it is practice, not the panel. The
panel's instance is drawn **in the panel's document, in place, in panel
coordinates**, from the understanding the study built; nothing is transferred,
and there is one coordinate space for marks. Measuring may still be per object:
`crop.py` cuts an object's crop out of the working-resolution subject at 1:1 and
stores its panel corner in the PNG, so its `trace.py` regions come back in panel
coordinates with no flag. `--zoom` is the magnified look a crop used to force.

```bash
python3 crop.py subject.png 200,560,460,540 meas/bike.png                # corner stored; trace.py reads it
node harness/cli.mjs DOC.json --only bike,frame --png bike.png          # the object alone, in the panel
```

`stroke` takes `tag=` for the object and for the edge alongside `stage=` for
the ladder phase, and `--only` / `--hide` match tags.

**Order the depth per tube, not per object.** "Draw the bike, then the figure" is
the wrong grain: the top tube passes in front of the far leg and the seat tube
passes behind the near one. An object is not at one depth, so it is written in
tagged depth groups, each interleaved with the other objects' groups.

**The interfaces need both objects present**, and in one document they always
are: draw each as the second of its two objects goes in, and give them the
budget the tier list saved.

Which parts get the 4x detail pass at their own stage 10: the one at the centre
of interest, and nothing else; the rest pass their own gates and stop.

## Splitting a scene across drawers [bought: drawing]

A panel divides badly by area and well by object: two drawers each taking half a
picture meet at a seam nobody owns, while one drawer per focus object meets at the
interfaces, which the panel has to name anyway.

**Studying and painting can be different drawers. Reading and drawing cannot.** The
drawer who paints must look at the subject itself; a handoff aims that looking and
never replaces it. Treat a handoff as a hypothesis with good pedigree: every
statement in one that proves wrong is caught by the painter re-measuring, and none
of it is catchable by trusting the page — including an asymmetry stated backwards,
which inverts a likeness while every box stays correct.

**What transfers**

- **Decisions**, expensive to reach and cheap to state: the light — including
  *there is none*, which is a measurement, not an omission — the value families and
  the empty gap between them, the depth order at every junction, what to leave out.
- **Numbers**: landmarks, the measured weight span, the palette with where each
  value was sampled, and the departure from the canon with the direction each
  feature is to be pushed — including which features are canonical and must *not* be
  pushed.
- **The census** (below).

**What does not transfer**

- **Boxes.** An inventory of bounding boxes cannot become a mark, and a box must
  never be drawn to. Hand over measured contours — the runs of points — or expect
  the outline to be measured twice.
- **Procedures.** A handoff passage that could be followed with the subject covered
  up is a scaffold, and it arrives precisely at the stage whose job is to find the
  particular form.
- **A weight ladder as numbers.** Each instrument has its own ladder and nothing in
  the numbers says so. The painting drawer draws and measures its own.

### The mechanics: sections, copies, merge [bought: one panel drawn this way]

A panel drawn by one drawer rationed its effort and left every part but the focus
at its S2 mass; the same panel drawn one drawer per part came back matching the
subject's dark area at 0.80 against 0.63, every part named by a blind viewer. The
procedure that did it:

1. **One drawer takes S0–S2 and the split.** Everything S0 and S2 produce, plus:
   - `draw.py` in depth order as **sections**, each opened by
     `# === part: NAME ===` or `# === welded: NAME ===` and closed by
     `# === end: NAME ===`, each holding that part's S2 mass lines.
   - **A section is a depth group, not an object.** An object that straddles
     another in depth — a far leg behind the frame, a near leg in front of it;
     rocks behind a wheel's spokes and rocks over its tyre — goes to the
     drawer of the thing it is interleaved with, or gets a second section
     (`part: rocks.back`). Three of six objects straddled on one panel.
   - `split.md`, per part: its keys and tag prefix, its box, its smallest
     feature, the sections before and after it, every interface row it is on
     and who is in front, its lost edges, its weight pitch, **the acceptance
     clauses it must make true, and which other drawers share each one** — a
     saddle whose only visible edge is the shorts' outline cannot be earned by
     the bicycle's drawer alone.
   - Each S2 mass is **tagged with every sub-form it stands in for**
     (`leg.near.thigh+leg.near.knee+leg.near.shin`), or the rows written at the
     crossing forms' grain have nothing to resolve against.
   - **Welded shapes are finished here, line and texture included**, because no
     later drawer owns them. A welded crest runs *under* the nearest part's crest
     ink, never above it: a part drawer can cover its own marks but not paint
     bare ground back, and a rock bed that overshot its pile in six places
     stayed wrong until the final pass. A welded shape may be several polygons —
     a far rock seen through spokes can be an island. A texture that stands on a
     welded shape's silhouette (grass tufts above a band's edge) is part of its
     outline, whatever the describer names.
2. **Each part drawer works in its own copy of the directory** and edits only
   between its own markers; a drawer that must fix a neighbour's section writes
   it in its gap log for the final pass instead. The part runs stages 2–10 with
   its own rounds. What it measures on its own zoom overrides S0's numbers — a
   census, a count of spokes — and it writes the correction into its entry. It
   may add rows to `parts.json` for its own sub-forms. It sets no round cap of
   its own and inherits none: a cap written into `reading.md` at S0 stopped a
   part drawer mid-campaign.
3. **Merge.** Replace each section of the master `draw.py` with the part's own;
   check first that every copy changed only its section. Merge `parts.json` **by
   entry**, not as text (a drawer may rewrite the whole file's whitespace), and
   flag a key two drawers changed differently. A palette name one drawer
   repointed is merged only when no other section draws with it. A mark that a
   part drawer found must sit in front of a later section (a hub nut in front of
   the fork) moves to a small section of its own after that one.
4. **Judge each part again on the merged panel.** Every part's crop held
   neighbours at S2 while it was drawn, so every plateau called in a copy is
   provisional: a critic shown those crops raised the stand-ins as the part's
   faults (5 of 6 items refuted), and `--ranking`, `--census` and `--parts` read
   mid-split are not verdicts. Show a part's critic `--only`/`--hide` renders of
   the part alone.
5. **One final drawer takes S4–S8 on the merged panel**, starting from the part
   drawers' notes about their neighbours. Then walk `--unfilled` in full: a bare
   triangle of jersey at the seam between two sections was the 18th of 30
   patches and nobody else saw it.

### The census — the check that catches an extra part

Every other instrument asks whether what was drawn is right. The census asks how
many there are. Before drawing, state the count of every countable thing, and
state the zeroes: no nostrils, no lips as shapes, no shadow on a face that carries
none. A missing part is caught by walking the inventory; a part drawn twice, a
third limb, a second shadow — nothing else catches those, because an extra part
has no box to be absent from.

### Accept a delegated drawing by its script, before looking at it

Run `check.py --ladder ops.json` on what comes back. A drawing that measured
honestly and skipped stages 6 and 8 comes back with its lines as filled shapes and
not one mark carrying its own taper, and it will pass every placement check here.
That is about *lines* — the edges of forms. A small form that is dark all
through, a droplet whose ink is wider than its interior, is a dark flat with its
interior written over it, and that is honest. `--ladder` also fails a drawing
whose marks were generated rather than written in its script; a script built by
concatenating generated sections is not a drawing, whatever its render passes.

### Budget, and why a self-reported one is not a budget

Studying one object to this depth and then painting it can cost more than an
entire flat panel once did, and a scene is as many of those as it has parts —
which is why the part list is fixed at stage 0 and why a scene with many
recognisable objects is expensive by construction rather than by accident.
Enforce the cap from outside the drawer, in checkpoints: after the masses,
after the first part, after the last. A drawer's own estimate of
what it has spent has come back low by more than a factor of two.

## Order of work

Steps 0–3 are the reversal. Nothing here draws an object.

0. **Read the source as a tone field.** Posterise it to three or four values and
   measure the **masses**, not the objects. Say in one sentence what the picture
   is about, and pick **one** centre of interest. *Done when* a thumbnail notan
   reads as a design with one dominant value and the strongest contrast at the
   focus.
1. **Armature.** Eye level, ground plane, the main perspective lines, the frame's
   subdivision, and the major masses' silhouettes. Still no objects. *Done when*
   every later shape can be located on this ground.
2. **Tonal plan and welding.** Give every region a value class and merge adjacent
   same-value objects into single shapes. The output is **five to twelve shapes,
   not eighty-four parts**. Record, per shape edge, **sharp or lost**. *Done
   when* the shape map matches the posterised source at thumbnail size and **no
   object has a closed contour**.
3. **Block the masses** in straights, lay the middle tone over the whole, then
   the light shapes, then the darks, honouring the sharp/lost decisions. *Done
   when* the downsampled render matches step 2.
4. **Every part: run the single-subject ladder**, unchanged, each in place in
   the panel's document, far to near, by depth group. The part
   at the centre of interest goes to the full quality bar; the others pass
   their gates at the lighter pitch decided in step 2 and stop.
5. **Emphasis gradient.** Decided before the parts are drawn and confirmed
   here: each part's weight pitch and edge count fall with distance from the
   centre of interest and with depth, and it was drawn that way. Nothing is
   added afterwards to make it so. *Done when* **no part
   beyond the focus carries the focus's weight** and every part is still named
   by a describer.
6. **Junction pass, as its own enumerated stage.** Walk every place two things
   meet; decide lost or found; put the occlusion accent where forms touch; break
   tangents by overlap or separation. *Done when* every junction has a recorded
   decision.
7. **Texture fields.** A few units drawn at the boundary nearest the focus, the
   rest as one indicated pattern.
8. **Spot the blacks and vignette.** One pass placing the black pattern across
   objects rather than within them; check the whole drawing's silhouette against
   the paper.
9. **Correct from a distance.** Re-downsample and compare against step 0, and
   confirm there is still exactly one focus.

**The stage gate means "no part has been SKIPPED at this stage" — not "every part
has had equal work".** At the masses gate a welded background band is *finished*
by its single shape while a focus object is only *begun*. Both are at the same
stage. Walk the inventory for omissions, not for equal depth.

**Budget by stage, not only by part.** Measured on a twelve-object panel to the
masses gate: **stage 0 — reading, measuring, the inventory and its blind
validation — 55%**; gesture/envelope/block-in 15%; construction 3%; masses plus
one correction round 27%. Stage 0 on a scene is more than half the job and should
be. Add a fixed cost the tier list cannot see: **loading this skill is itself a
material fraction of a scene's budget**, and both drawers on that panel spent
roughly a third of their run reading before a mark existed.
