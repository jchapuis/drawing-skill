# Drawing a scene

A scene is a different problem from a bigger subject. A single subject of about
twenty named parts comes out coherent. A scene of seventy or eighty parts comes
out with every part thin: a garment that is a balloon, a hip with no joint in
it, a tyre that never closes. None of these is a mistake in itself. The cause is
not that the marks ran out, and giving each part more marks makes the picture
busier, not better.

The cause is that nothing organised the picture above the level of the object
[read: source]. Loomis: *"More pictures are bad because no attempt at tone
organization has been made than for any other reason"*, and he names the symptom:
*"tones so scattered and broken up as not to have the much-needed impact"*. A
picture of eighty equally shallow parts fails for want of a dominant tone and a
single centre of interest, not for want of work.

So design the picture as one thing before you draw it as many.

One thing does scale with the part count, and it is the eye. A render is looked
at at a fixed size, so a feature that is 10% of a single-object crop is 1-3% of
the whole picture, below what can be judged as a shape. That is why every focus
object is looked at magnified (`--zoom`) and drawn at a working resolution its
smallest feature needs. This is separate from the design problem: an object drawn
well inside an undesigned picture still breaks the picture.

## The mass is the unit, and objects are named afterwards [read: source]

Every painting tradition reaches the same order of work.

Loomis, on where to start: *"It is better, actually, to think of the setting
before you think of the figure."* And on the working procedure: *"make some
patterns of dark (eventually rocks), some greys (eventually shadow or sky), and
some whites (eventually clouds). We soon find the spot for the bathing girl."*
The rocks are not drawn and then toned. A dark shape is placed, and it is called
a rock later.

On speed: *"The reducing of a complicated appearance to a few simple masses is
the first necessity of the painter"*, and his block-in is explicitly *"not really
a drawing at all, but a species of mapping out"*. Carolus-Duran's atelier taught
that a painter *"does not consider it advisable to finish each bit at a time,
but prefers that all the tones should first be blocked in very coarsely"*.
Gurney states it as an instruction: merge adjacent tonal shapes into larger
abstract patterns, after Pyle: *"Put your white against white, middle tones
against grays, black against black, then black and white where you want your
center of interest."*

Loomis also names the failure of the opposite order: finishing a figure and then
*"trying to fill the spaces left around it"* with background *"usually results in
[poor] arrangement or none at all."*

### When does an object get to exist?

Two rules decide it:

- **Distance from the one centre of interest.** Guptill: *"the strongest
  contrasts and sharpest details appear at this center of interest, and grow
  less and less distinct towards the edges of the paper"*, and *"be careful not
  to have two or more centers showing equal emphasis."*
- **Contrast with its own surround.** An object earns a contour by differing in
  value from the mass it sits in. Otherwise it stays inside the mass and is never
  drawn as a thing. Loomis: *"surface detail should become submerged in tone as
  it goes back… the distance can become almost poster in effect."*

## Most edges are lost, and edges belong to the mass stage

Edges are decided at the mass stage, not at the contour stage. This is where scenes have
actually failed, and the literature does not treat edges as a side problem.

Loomis: *"Contour cannot be continuously defined all around all units and a
sense of space be achieved… If the edge is kept hard all around, it cannot avoid
sticking to the picture plane"*, and *"perhaps the most important element in
obtaining freedom and looseness is found in the treatment of edges."* Speed
observes lost and found edges at the mass stage, before any contour exists.
Pyle's rule has a consequence: the majority of junctions in a scene should be
lost.

Running the object stages on each object separately does the opposite. It gives
every object a closed contour of its own, so every junction is found against
found at equal weight, with no occlusion accent and no stage at which the
junction itself was the subject. Two neighbouring objects then meet as two
complete outlines that cross. In one test, the marks that crossed a group of
neighbouring objects were not stray construction. They were the second copy of every
shared edge, drawn once as one object's silhouette and again as its neighbour's,
from two different guesses.

Two facts about edges [read: source]. The sharpest shadow is where one surface
touches another, so contact points carry the darkest accent in the picture, and
that accent is what makes a thing sit on the ground. Two edges that only graze
each other (a tangent) read as spatial ambiguity. Fix a tangent with a decisive
overlap or a clear separation, never a near miss.

Losing an edge is also the only cheap way to push a competing object back. A
translucent glaze over an object that is too loud does not push it back a plane.
It makes a dirty copy of it in place. To recede an object, remove its contour and
let it merge into the mass behind it.

## The budget is a design decision, not a division

Tiers are masses, not ranked objects. Membership is decided by contrast with the
surround and by distance from the one centre of interest, not by semantic
importance. The furthest tier gets no marks of its own: it lives inside a mass
and receives at most one found edge.

"State it in the fewest confident marks" is the wrong instruction. Few marks per
object still gives a closed contour per object, and that is what breaks the
picture. The instruction is no silhouette, not fewer marks.

A middle tier ("stated": only the edges that differ, drawn at the picture's
scale) was tried and is refuted. In one test every object given that treatment
came out crude, the furniture as much as the figure: a lamp becomes a diamond on a stick, a
strip of tape becomes four floating squares, a torso becomes one bean with no shoulder,
while an object drawn magnified at four times the picture's scale comes out well.
The eye that draws at the picture's scale is the eye that cannot see at that
scale, and no economy of marks changes that. So there are two tiers:

| tier | what it is | treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **part** | Every object that must be recognisable (the figure, the vehicle, the lamp, the picture on the wall), each an object and never a feature of one | The single-subject stages, in the picture's one document at the working resolution its smallest feature needs, looked at with `--zoom`. Emphasis is the line weight and edge count it is drawn with, decided at stage 0. A far part is drawn lighter and sparer from the start, not thinned afterwards |
| **mass** | Everything else, which is most of the list | Inside a mass. No contour and no marks of its own, except in a source that inks every flat boundary, where the mass keeps its one line (`redrawing.md`) |

The cost has no shortcut: a figure or a vehicle is a whole single-subject run,
and a lamp is a tenth of one. A named object is either paid for or merged into a
mass, and the reading decides which before any part is begun.

### Named devices for what is not rendered [read: source]

Every tradition has specific techniques for the fact that you cannot render
everything:

- **Tonal plan / notan**: one dominant tone and two to four value masses for the
  whole picture, decided before anything is drawn.
- **Shape welding** (Gurney's term): adjacent same-value objects fused into one
  shape, with objects lost in shadow.
- **Finish gradient from one centre.** Guptill: *"start at the center of
  interest and work out from there, continuing the sketch only until the drawing
  includes enough area and is sufficiently rendered to properly convey its
  message."*
- **Indication**: half an outline still reads. *"To suggest or indicate brings
  more interesting and more artistic results… than does tiresomely complete
  outline."*
- **Texture fields.** Foliage, crowds and brick are a pattern studied once and
  applied as a field, never enumerated as objects. A field the describer names (a
  rock field, a crowd) is still a part, because the acceptance list will ask for
  it. Draw it with this device: its sub-form list is the unit types and the
  units at the boundary nearest the focus, which get full treatment, and the
  rest are flats with a silhouette and no interior line. On a flat-cel source
  that inks every boundary, the far units' missing interior ink is a departure to
  write down, not a broken rule. A faceted field (rocks, crystals, folded paper)
  is straights throughout: set `smooth=False` on every stroke, because rendered
  smooth, a rock field became river stones and every gate still passed. Inking
  every face's closed outline states each shared edge twice (`--doubled` went
  from 20 to 29 on one field). Ink a unit's silhouette once and its interior face
  edges as open strokes, each shared edge written once from the point dict.
- **Distance to poster**: fewer planes, less halftone and flatter light the
  further back, with line weight thinning with depth.
- **Vignette**: the drawing fades into the paper, and the silhouette the whole
  drawing makes against the paper is itself a designed shape.
- **Two treatments on purpose**: the comics' masking effect, with iconic figures
  against a realistic setting, deliberately not reconciled.

### Where the traditions disagree

Comics, bande dessinée and manga do work object by object, by different hands. A
mangaka draws the characters while assistants do the backgrounds, and Studios
Hergé had one artist for décors and another for vehicles. Animation is the
extreme: characters and backgrounds are different artists in different media.

That is labour divided inside a scene-level design, not a counterexample. A
layout fixes perspective and placement before anyone draws, the two treatments
are chosen deliberately, and a late unifying pass spots the blacks across the
whole image. Nobody starts from eighty-four parts.

## Overlaps are objects, and they are where scenes fail

An overlap is a place where two forms touch or cross. Typical failures:

| overlap | how it fails |
|---|---|
| hand / handle | one fused dark mass, where the subject separates them with a band of its own ground or a second lighter value |
| foot / pedal | two discs, until a sole and a strap slot go in |
| hip / seat | no joint at all, with supports leaving the bottom of a black shape |
| limb / tube behind it | the tube's ink runs straight over the limb in front |
| wheel / floor | the ring never closes, so nothing bears weight |
| shoulder / arm | no upper arm; the forearm starts at the garment's edge |

- **Put the overlaps in `parts.json` as named parts with their own boxes.** They
  are exactly the gap between two boxes, and no check looks there unless it is
  named.
- **Draw the overlap at the same time as both objects it joins.** A hand drawn
  onto a finished bar looks stuck on.
- **Occlusion is order.** Marks render in the order they are written, and a flat
  is opaque, so a nearer group's fills laid after a farther group's ink cover it
  exactly where the near form is. Write each depth group as its fills then its
  ink, back to front. Do not send every fill to the back of the scene: then a
  flat cannot hide ink and every far contour has to be cut by hand. What remains
  to draw at the crossing is the accent where the two forms touch.
- **Decide the depth order per crossing in writing, at stage 0**, in an `in front`
  column beside the overlap itself. No mark records it (a stroke has no z, and
  the checks cannot ask). Without it, the order is settled by whichever mark
  happened to be written last, which is how a chainring ends up drawn across the
  shoe standing on it.
- **One edge, one stroke.** Where two objects share a boundary, draw that boundary
  once, as one object's, and stop the other object's contour at it. An edge drawn
  twice from two guesses is the commonest mark-level fault in a scene.

If you skip the depth order, two objects that cross come out as one black mass.
The cause is usually not value, since both are often the same colour in the
subject too. The far object's marks were drawn over the near one and nothing
decided they were behind it. Once the decision exists, the fix is the order the
marks are written in, and it is exact by construction. `check.py --depth` reads
it. Never judge a crossing at 1:1, because one that is a few pixels wrong and one
that is right look identical there.

## Studies, and how they reduce the budget [read: comics and atelier practice]

A scene cannot afford to build eighty parts. The profession's answer is the
study, a standard pipeline: thumbnails settle the composition before anything is
drawn at size. Elements are worked on a separate sheet and traced through. Roughs
are printed as bluelines on the final sheet and drawn over, never redrawn from
memory. The oldest version is the Renaissance *cartone*, worked until it is
right, then pricked and pounced onto the wall.

It works because an object drawn on its own is a small subject again, and gets a
small subject's attention however many parts the picture has. The transfer step
does not survive here: objects drawn in their own crop and composited back came
out as blobs, while the same objects drawn in place came out well. What survives
is the study and the magnified look.

### A study builds the capacity to draw the thing, not one copy of it

A study is the same form drawn several times, off the picture, from different
angles, until the form is understood as a solid. Only then is the picture's
instance drawn, from that understanding and not from the reference's outline.

If you draw it once, in place, at the angle the picture needs, you have a traced
silhouette. It records the 2D boundary from one angle and contradicts nothing, so
it has no way to be wrong, which is the same as no way to be corrected. The test
is mechanical: if you cannot draw the object at an angle the reference does not
show, you have not studied it, you have copied it.

### Where a study lives, and how the picture gets it

A study goes in its own document, because it is practice and not the picture. The
picture's instance is drawn in the picture's document, in place, in the picture's
coordinates, from the understanding the study built. Nothing is transferred, and
there is one coordinate space for marks. Measuring can still be per object:
`crop.py` cuts an object's crop out of the working-resolution subject at 1:1 and
stores its corner in the PNG, so its `trace.py` regions come back in the
picture's coordinates with no flag. `--zoom` gives the magnified look.

```bash
python3 crop.py subject.png 200,560,460,540 meas/bike.png                # corner stored; trace.py reads it
node harness/cli.mjs DOC.json --only bike,frame --png bike.png          # the object alone, in the picture
```

`stroke` takes `tag=` for the object and for the edge, and `stage=` for the
stage, and `--only` / `--hide` match tags.

Order the depth per tube, not per object. "Draw the bike, then the figure" is
the wrong grain: the top tube passes in front of the far leg and the seat tube
passes behind the near one. An object is not at one depth, so write it in tagged
depth groups, each interleaved with the other objects' groups.

The overlaps need both objects present, and in one document they always are. Draw
each overlap as the second of its two objects goes in, and give overlaps the
budget that the tiers saved.

Only the part at the centre of interest gets the 4x detail pass at its own stage
10. The rest pass their own gates and stop.

## Splitting a scene across agents

Divide a picture by object, not by area. Two agents each taking half a picture
meet at a seam nobody owns, while one agent per focus object meets at the
overlaps, which the picture has to name anyway.

Studying and painting can be done by different agents. Reading and drawing cannot.
The agent that paints must look at the subject itself. A handoff aims that
looking and never replaces it. Treat a handoff as a hypothesis from a source that
is usually right: every statement in it that proves wrong is caught when the
painter re-measures, and none of it is caught by trusting the page. That includes
an asymmetry stated backwards, which inverts a likeness while every box stays
correct.

**What transfers**

- **Decisions**, which are expensive to reach and cheap to state: the light
  (including *there is none*, which is a measurement and not an omission), the
  value groups and the empty gap between them, the depth order at every junction,
  and what to leave out.
- **Numbers**: landmarks, the measured weight span, the palette with where each
  value was sampled, and how this subject differs from the typical one, with the
  direction each feature is to be pushed. Say also which features are canonical
  and must not be pushed.
- **The count** (below).

**What does not transfer**

- **Boxes.** An inventory of bounding boxes cannot become a mark, and a box must
  never be drawn to. Hand over measured contours (the runs of points), or expect
  the outline to be measured twice.
- **Procedures.** A handoff passage that could be followed with the subject
  covered up is a scaffold, and it arrives at the stage whose job is to find the
  particular form.
- **A weight swatch as numbers.** Each instrument has its own weights and nothing
  in the numbers says so. The agent that paints draws and measures its own
  swatch.

### The mechanics: sections, copies, merge

Splitting by part gives every part full attention. In one test, a scene drawn by one agent that rationed its effort left every part
but the focus at its S2 mass; the same scene drawn by one agent per part matched
the subject's dark area at 0.80 against 0.63, and a blind viewer named every part.
The procedure:

1. **One agent takes S0-S2 and the split.** It produces everything S0 and S2
   produce, plus:
   - `draw.py` in depth order as **sections**, each opened by
     `# === part: NAME ===` or `# === mass: NAME ===` and closed by
     `# === end: NAME ===`, each holding that part's S2 mass lines.
   - **A section is a depth group, not an object.** An object that straddles
     another in depth goes to the agent of the thing it is interleaved with, or
     gets a second section (`part: rocks.back`). Examples: a far leg behind a
     frame and a near leg in front of it; rocks behind a wheel's spokes and rocks
     over its tyre. In one test, three of six objects straddled.
   - `split.md`, per part: its keys and tag prefix, its box, its smallest
     feature, the sections before and after it, every overlap row it is on and
     who is in front, its lost edges, its emphasis, the acceptance clauses it
     must make true, and which other agents share each one. A saddle whose only
     visible edge is the shorts' outline cannot be earned by the bicycle's agent
     alone.
   - Each S2 mass is **tagged with every sub-form it stands in for**
     (`leg.near.thigh+leg.near.knee+leg.near.shin`), or the rows written at the
     crossing forms' grain have nothing to resolve against.
   - **Masses are finished here, line and texture included**, because no later
     agent owns them. A mass's crest runs under the nearest part's crest ink,
     never above it: a part's agent can cover its own marks but cannot paint bare
     ground back, and a rock bed that overshoots its pile stays wrong until the
     final pass. A mass may be several polygons (a far rock seen through spokes
     can be an island). A texture that stands on a mass's silhouette (grass tufts
     above a band's edge) is part of its outline, whatever the describer names.
2. **Each part's agent works in its own copy of the directory** and edits only
   between its own markers. An agent that must fix a neighbour's section writes
   it in its gap log for the final pass instead. The part runs stages 2-10 with
   its own rounds. What it measures on its own zoom overrides S0's numbers (a
   count, a number of spokes), and it writes the correction into its entry. It may
   add rows to `parts.json` for its own sub-forms. It sets no round cap of its own
   and inherits none: a cap written into `reading.md` at S0 can stop a part's
   agent mid-campaign.
3. **Merge.** Replace each section of the master `draw.py` with the part's own,
   after checking that every copy changed only its section. Merge `parts.json` by
   entry, not as text (an agent may rewrite the whole file's whitespace), and flag
   a key two agents changed differently. A palette name one agent added is merged
   as is; one an agent re-sampled is merged only when no other section draws with it. A mark that a part's agent
   found must sit in front of a later section (a hub nut in front of the fork)
   moves to a small section of its own after that one.
4. **Judge each part again on the merged picture.** Every part's crop held
   neighbours at S2 while it was drawn, so every plateau called in a copy is
   provisional. A critic shown those crops raises the stand-ins as the part's
   faults. `--ranking`, `--counts` and `--parts` read mid-split are not verdicts.
   Show a part's critic `--only`/`--hide` renders of the part alone.
5. **One final agent takes S4-S8 on the merged picture**, starting from the part
   agents' notes about their neighbours. Then walk `--unfilled` in full: a bare
   triangle of jersey at the seam between two sections is the kind of patch
   nobody else sees.

### The count: the check that catches an extra part

Every other instrument asks whether what was drawn is right. The count asks how
many there are. Before drawing, state the count of every countable thing, and
state the zeroes: no nostrils, no lips as shapes, no shadow on a face that
carries none. A missing part is caught by walking the inventory. A part drawn
twice, a third limb or a second shadow is caught by nothing else, because an
extra part has no box to be absent from.

### Accept a delegated drawing by its script, before looking at it

Run `check.py --stages ops.json` on what comes back. A drawing that measured
honestly and skipped stages 6 and 8 comes back with its lines as filled shapes
and not one mark carrying its own taper, and it passes every placement check.
That concerns lines, the edges of forms. A small form that is dark all through
(a droplet whose ink is wider than its interior) is a dark flat with its interior
written over it, and that is honest. `--stages` also fails a drawing whose marks
were generated rather than written in its script. A script built by concatenating
generated sections is not a drawing, whatever its render passes.

### Budget, and why a self-reported one is not a budget

Studying one object to this depth and then painting it can cost more than an
entire flat picture, and a scene is as many of those as it has parts. That is why
the part list is fixed at stage 0, and why a scene with many recognisable objects
is expensive by construction. Enforce the cap from outside the agent, in
checkpoints: after the masses, after the first part, after the last. An agent's
own estimate of what it has spent can come back low by more than a factor of two.

## Order of work

Steps 0-3 reverse the usual order. Nothing in them draws an object.

0. **Read the source as a tone field.** Posterise it to three or four values and
   measure the masses, not the objects. Say in one sentence what the picture is
   about, and pick one centre of interest. *Done when* a thumbnail notan reads as
   a design with one dominant value and the strongest contrast at the focus.
1. **Armature.** Eye level, ground plane, the main perspective lines, the frame's
   subdivision, and the major masses' silhouettes. Still no objects. *Done when*
   every later shape can be located on this ground.
2. **Tonal plan and merging.** Give every region a value class and merge adjacent
   same-value objects into single shapes. The output is five to twelve shapes,
   not eighty-four parts. Record, per shape edge, sharp or lost. *Done when* the
   shape map matches the posterised source at thumbnail size and no object has a
   closed contour.
3. **Block the masses** in straights, lay the middle tone over the whole, then the
   light shapes, then the darks, honouring the sharp/lost decisions. *Done when*
   the downsampled render matches step 2.
4. **Every part: run the single-subject stages**, unchanged, each in place in the
   picture's document, far to near, by depth group. The part at the centre of
   interest goes to the full quality bar. The others pass their gates at the
   lighter emphasis decided in step 2 and stop.
5. **Emphasis gradient.** Decide it before the parts are drawn and confirm it
   here: each part's line weight and edge count fall with distance from the
   centre of interest and with depth, and each part was drawn that way. Nothing is
   added afterwards to make it so. *Done when* no part beyond the focus carries
   the focus's weight and every part is still named by a describer.
6. **Junction pass, as its own enumerated stage.** Walk every place two things
   meet, decide lost or found, put the occlusion accent where forms touch, and
   break tangents by overlap or separation. *Done when* every junction has a
   recorded decision.
7. **Texture fields.** A few units drawn at the boundary nearest the focus, the
   rest as one indicated pattern.
8. **Spot the blacks and vignette.** One pass placing the black pattern across
   objects rather than within them, then a check of the whole drawing's silhouette
   against the paper.
9. **Correct from a distance.** Re-downsample and compare against step 0, and
   confirm there is still exactly one focus.

The stage gate means that no part has been skipped at this stage. It does not
mean every part has had equal work. At the masses gate a background band is
finished by its single shape while a focus object is only begun, and both are at
the same stage. Walk the inventory for omissions, not for equal depth.

**Budget by stage, not only by part.** On a twelve-object scene taken to the
masses gate, the split was: stage 0 (reading, measuring, the inventory and its
blind validation) 55%; gesture, envelope and block-in 15%; construction 3%;
masses plus one correction round 27%. Stage 0 on a scene is more than half the
job, and should be. Add a fixed cost the tiers do not show: loading this skill is
itself a material fraction of a scene's budget, and in one test both agents spent
roughly a third of their run reading before a mark existed.
